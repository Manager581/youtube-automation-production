Right now, on a bare rock in the Pacific, this seabird is being drained — one drop at a time.

Not by a shark. Not by a storm. But by a bird small enough to sit in the palm of your hand.

And out here, there is nowhere left to run.

It has tried everything — shaking them off, snapping, twisting away.

They simply hop back on.

Every hour, a little more of its blood is carried off — and the great bird just... endures it.

As if being slowly emptied were somehow the safer choice.

This is a ground finch. A seed-eater — the same unremarkable little bird Darwin watched cracking seeds on these islands, two centuries ago.

It has no business wanting blood at all.

So how did a seed-eater learn to drink blood — and why does a seabird twenty times its size simply let it happen?

It works the base of a growing feather, where the blood runs closest to the skin...

And for the first time in days, the seabird goes perfectly still.

It did not begin as cruelty.

Long ago, these finches earned their place on the boobies' backs — picking off the ticks and biting flies the big birds could never reach themselves. A fair trade. The seabird got clean; the finch got fed.

But every so often, a beak broke skin. A bead of blood welled up... and the finch drank.

And a bird starving on a bare rock does not forget a taste like that.

Slowly, over generations, the cleaner became the parasite. Today, close to a tenth of everything this finch eats is blood.

It still cannot make its own water. It still cannot cross the open sea. Everything it needs, it must take — from the only large, warm, living thing for miles.

Because these are two of the most remote islands in all the Galápagos. Wolf. And Darwin.

No streams. No soft ground. For half of every year, the rain simply stops — and the seeds run out.

So the finch drinks whatever it can find. Nectar from a cactus flower.

Seawater. Even the droppings of other birds.

And when there is nothing else left at all.

There is always blood.

On an island full of seabirds, it is the one thing that never runs dry.

But there is a deeper reason the seabird will not simply stand up and leave.

Beneath it, in the dust, is a single egg.

And these same finches are egg-thieves.

The instant the booby lifts off to escape the bleeding.

They take the very thing it was trying to protect.

So it makes the only choice the island allows. It stays. It shields the egg. And it lets them drink.

By morning, it is quiet again.

The finches settle back onto the bird they feed on, as though nothing had passed between them.

The seabird keeps its egg. The finch keeps its life.

Out here, at the far edge of the world, survival is not kindness — and it is not cruelty.

It is only the bargain the island allows...

Paid one drop of blood at a time.
