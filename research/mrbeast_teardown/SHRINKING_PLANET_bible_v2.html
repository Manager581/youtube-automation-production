What the review caught in v1

Verdict on v1: not ready. The reviewer's exact charge: the outline was "a scene-by-scene transplant of the #1 video, not a synthesis of laws." It was right. Here is what was wrong and what changed — shown so you can see the mistake-catching working, instead of finding these yourself after credits were spent.

  • Budget built on a misread number. v1 used "31 of 88 clips banked" as a 35% yield. That is a progress counter, not a reject rate. Real roll-1 pass rates on record: Dinoverse zone 9 = 9/10, Christ Cares = 7/7, cookies = 36/36 held identity. v2 plans at 80–90% roll-1 with a 3-roll cap.
  • Wrong throughput model, real cap ignored. v1 used "20 clips/day." The proven recipe runs ~36 clips/hour; the limit that has actually stopped production here is Grok's weekly cap (~160 clips/week observed). v2 budgets in weekly cycles.
  • Clip counts contradicted themselves 2× (beats summed to 88, budget said 156) and the budget only balanced by re-using one drone loop 40 times — which violates the channel's own clip-variety rule. v2 counts unique clips per beat under that rule: ~176.
  • Story logic broke twice. The planet ladder hit 0 m on Day 29 instead of 2 m; and "crossing the red line = quit, both lose" collided with "launching the pod = steal everything" on the same object. v2 separates the devices (red line = quit; launch key = steal) and fixes the ladder: 20 → 12 → 7 → 4 → 2 m.
  • 25+ lines lifted near-verbatim from the #1 video while claiming an original premise. Every line of dialogue below is rewritten. The structure is borrowed on purpose (that is the point of a teardown); the words are not.
  • Six of fourteen "laws" were overstated by their own evidence lists. Counts below are the honest ones (e.g. "$ figure in the first 17 s" is 6 of 10, not 8; the "genre flip at 64–85%" window is 3 of 10, though 8 of 10 announce a format change somewhere).
  • Hook targets quoted the wrong measurements (60-second and loose-threshold counts presented as 45-second hard cuts). Corrected numbers in the hook-grammar section.
  • PIP's line count broke the budget's own cap (~30 mouth-visible lines vs a ≤20 cap set because talking-creature lip-sync is this repo's biggest known failure). v2 keeps PIP's on-camera mouth lines ≤20 and carries the rest as voice-over on reactions and inserts.
  • Script length ~30% low. The references run 197–213 words per minute. A 20-minute episode is ~4,000 words ≈ 24k characters (≈30k with retake reserve), delivered in ~6 chunks — not one pass.
  • The "AI faces" rule was mischaracterized. That memory is a quality bar about a real person's likeness, not a ban on AI humans. The creature cast is kept — but as a strategic choice (the repo's proven strength + lip-sync tolerance), not rule compliance. ORB also lost its "two tiny arms" (an uncosted consistency risk across 40 gestures); objects now arrive on tray drones.
  • The prop-money contradiction. v1 said "don't pretend the money is real" and then put $1,000,000 in 4 of 5 titles. v2's default title sells survival, not cash; the in-world prize is fictional and disclosed. If you'd rather fund a real giveaway, money titles become honest — that is your Gate 0 call.
  • "One-off episode" contradicted the series mascots. v2 commits to a series-capable cast; the sameness defense is a different premise every episode, not a single upload.
  • Three wrong citations fixed (a running-gag count, a consolation-money citation that paid losers not winners, and a flash-forward that spoiled the finale).
  • Two tool leaks struck: Remotion (not in this repo — the engine is the FFmpeg renderer) and an unnamed "upscaler" (720p→1080p is a plain ffmpeg scale pass, stated as such).

Where the all-AI idea collides with reality

Pushback you asked for. Each problem carries its mitigation; two need your decision at Gate 0.

1 · The authenticity engine is gone.

All ten videos run on real strangers with real jobs and 6 of 10 open with a real dollar figure. An AI cast cannot receive money, so "will he steal it?" has no real referent. Mitigation: don't pretend. Declare it an animated game show (AI disclosure on every upload), and build stakes fiction can honor: visible, irreversible world-loss (the planet literally shrinks — the one thing only AI can stage at this scale) and a relationship stake that pays off regardless of money. Gate 0 decision keep the prize fictional and sell survival in the title, or fund a real viewer giveaway and earn the right to a money title.

2 · Human faces at a 1.25-second cut rhythm.

Photoreal AI humans read uncanny at hook density and lip-sync fails frame QA. Mitigation: zero photoreal humans. A creature cast with strong silhouettes and mouths that tolerate imperfect sync — a mouthless host, a fur-covered mouth, and one small mouth used sparingly. This plays to what this repo has actually proven it can render consistently (creatures), and it is a choice, not a rule.

3 · Sameness is the demonetization trigger, not AI.

A beat-for-beat clone with an AI cast is the definition of a template farm. Mitigation: copy the laws, not the show — original premise, original characters and names, a locked art style, hand-built graphics through the existing FFmpeg renderer, a different premise every episode, and a per-upload edit-fingerprint check against the reference.

4 · Likeness.

No name, logo, wordmark, snack brands, no bearded brown-haired human host, no "you're in a [Name] video" phrasing. The genre grammar ("Survive X, Win Y", "Last To Leave") is generic and stays.

5 · Credits are the real risk.

A 20-minute all-generated video is ~176 unique clips before rejects. Mitigation: stage-gated spend — the hook prototype (≈14 clips) is generated first and measured against the hook numbers above before any act is touched; the five hero environment shots are generated next because they are re-used most; then act by act, each reviewed on a contact sheet before the next act's clips exist.

6 · Scripted animation cannot surprise the writer.

The references are carried by genuinely unscripted outcomes. Mitigation: script the expectation violations explicitly — the unchosen platter revealed as the free option, the trust beam that deliberately leaves one answer unverified, a finale whose answer the truth device withholds. Runtime stays at 20:00 (the references' ~4 minutes of sponsor slots are cut; this channel has none).

7 · Same creature, every shot, for "30 days."

This repo's largest recorded burn was a subject swap. Mitigation: SUBJECT LOCK before spend — one approved seed per character, no costume changes across the 30 days (only the planet changes), every clip generated from composites of those masters, frame-strip QA per clip, contact-sheet review per act.

Cast · four originals

Host

ORB

A 60-cm floating chrome sphere with one large glass lens-eye and a gold ring-light halo that pulses when it speaks. No mouth, no arms. Always the same starfield reflection. Objects it "hands over" arrive on tray drones. Why: no face and no lip-sync to fail; speech is a halo pulse (a brightness keyframe in FFmpeg); readable silhouette at 1-second shot lengths; a host that can leave the planet — the shot no real camera can buy.

Contestant · magenta

PIP

A 1-m axolotl-like creature, matte magenta skin, six frilly gill-fronds that act as hair and emote (droop = sad, flare = angry, curl = flirt), oversized glossy black eyes, one yellow scarf that never changes. Small mouth. Why: emotion carried by fronds and eyes, not lips; mouth-visible lines capped at 20 for the whole episode, the rest voice-over on reactions. She is the singer who went solo — the emotional lead.

Contestant · cyan

GRUFF

A 2.5-m cyan-blue yeti whose long fur completely covers the mouth; speech reads as fur movement and heavy brows. One cracked brass goggle worn on the forehead — the plot object. No clothes. Why: a fur-covered mouth is the cheapest talking creature possible; his size makes the 2-metre-planet finale (the thumbnail) absurd on sight. He is the drummer who got left behind.

Scoreboard

THE COUNT

A matte-black hovering 40-cm cube with red seven-segment displays on every face, one wobbling antenna, four thrusters. No face, no voice — beeps. Numbers are composited over its clean plate. Why: makes the number ladder diegetic and nearly free; a comic foil that malfunctions; used as inserts (never adjacent to itself) to respect the variety rule.
