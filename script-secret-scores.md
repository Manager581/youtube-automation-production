# YOU HAVE A SECRET SCORE (AND IT'S RUINING YOUR LIFE)
## Full Narration Script | ~20 minutes at 138.7 WPM

---

Mary Louis did everything right. Sixteen years in the same apartment. Never missed a rent payment. Not once. When she applied to move to Granada Highlands in Malden, Massachusetts, she had a housing voucher that guaranteed nearly seventy percent of her rent would be paid directly by a government agency. On paper, she was the perfect tenant. But she never got to show anyone that paper — because a computer said no.

An algorithm she'd never heard of, built by a company she'd never interacted with, generated a number. A score. And that score decided she wasn't worthy of a home. Not because she'd ever been evicted. Not because she couldn't pay. Because the algorithm didn't know how to count a housing voucher as income. It looked at her credit history, saw non-tenancy debts — a medical bill, an old phone balance — and spat out a number that erased sixteen years of proof that Mary Louis pays her rent.

Here's what should disturb you: Mary Louis isn't an edge case. She's the system working exactly as designed.

Because right now, as you're watching this, there is a score attached to your name that you have never seen. You didn't consent to it. You can't dispute it. And it is quietly deciding whether you get an apartment, whether you get a job, and how much you pay for insurance. It is not your credit score. Your credit score — you at least know about that. This is something else entirely. This is a shadow score. And the industry that builds these scores is worth billions of dollars precisely because you don't know it exists.

Let me show you how deep this goes.

---

You'd think that if a company was going to decide whether you deserve housing, there would be rules. Regulations. Oversight. And technically, there are. The Fair Credit Reporting Act was passed in 1970 to make sure that when someone pulls your credit report, the information is accurate, and if you're denied something, you get told why.

But here's the problem. The FCRA was written for a world where a human being opened a file folder and read a credit report. It was not written for a world where an algorithm trained on thirty million lease outcomes generates a risk score in milliseconds using data you didn't even know was being collected.

The tenant screening industry alone is now worth nearly two billion dollars. Roughly two thousand companies sell screening software. Nine out of ten landlords use it. And the biggest players — SafeRent, RealPage, TransUnion SmartMove — they don't just check your credit. They pull your eviction history, your criminal records, your debt patterns, your rent-to-income ratio, and then they feed all of it into a proprietary algorithm that produces a single number. That number gets sent to the landlord. And only three percent of renters even know which company screened them.

Think about that. Ninety-seven percent of people who get screened for an apartment have no idea who judged them.

So what happens when the algorithm gets it wrong?

Ask Marco Antonio Fernandez. He's an active-duty Navy serviceman with a top-secret security clearance, coming home from a year-long deployment in South Korea. He applied for an apartment near Fort Meade, Maryland. The screening software rejected him. The reason? It said he had a drug conviction and three misdemeanors for petty theft. A man trusted by the United States military with classified intelligence was denied an apartment because the algorithm confused him with Marco Alberto Fernandez Santana — an alleged Mexican drug trafficker. Different middle name. Different date of birth. Different person entirely. But the algorithm matched them anyway.

Or ask William Hall Junior, who was denied housing in Georgia because TransUnion's screening system reported that he had sexually abused a minor. The criminal record actually belonged to a different William Hall — one who was thirty years older and possibly dead. But the algorithm doesn't care about age gaps or context. It matches names and moves on.

These aren't glitches. This is how the system works. RealPage's own screening product was caught matching "Anthony Jones" to "Antoinette Jones." One background check company produced eleven thousand inaccurate renter reports between 2014 and 2019. And because eighty-seven percent of criminal records in New York contain at least one error, and sixty-nine percent are missing outcome data entirely, the algorithms are building risk profiles on a foundation of broken information.

But surely, you're thinking, if you get denied because of bad data, you can fight it. You can dispute the error and get it fixed. Right?

In theory, yes. In practice, the system is designed to make that nearly impossible. The CFPB analyzed twenty-six thousand tenant screening complaints over four years. More than sixteen thousand were about incorrect information. Another forty-five hundred were about obstacles people hit when trying to fix the errors. And remember — the consumer protection agency that was supposed to oversee all of this, the CFPB, was effectively gutted in February 2025. Roughly fifteen hundred of its seventeen hundred employees were fired. Active lawsuits against major banks were dropped. As of right now, the algorithmic oversight division is a ghost.

So the cops are gone. And the companies scoring you are getting bigger.

---

Let's talk about who's actually building these scores, because it's not a handful of scrappy startups. It's some of the largest private equity firms on the planet.

Thoma Bravo — a firm managing over a hundred and eighty-three billion dollars in assets — acquired RealPage for ten point two billion dollars in 2021. RealPage manages screening and pricing algorithms for more than twenty-four million housing units. That's roughly one in every six rental apartments in America. After the acquisition, they took the company private. Which means no more SEC filings. No more public financial disclosures. No more transparency.

The Carlyle Group, managing four hundred and twenty-six billion dollars, bought HireVue — the company that uses AI to evaluate job candidates in video interviews. HireVue has processed over seventy million video interviews and two hundred million chat-based candidate interactions. More than sixty percent of the Fortune 100 uses it.

Stone Point Capital and Insight Partners acquired CoreLogic — the company behind the CrimSAFE screening product — for five point nine billion dollars. They took it private too, then rebranded it to Cotality in 2025 with the tagline "Intelligence beyond bounds." I couldn't make that up.

See the pattern? Step one: acquire a company that holds enormous power over people's lives. Step two: take it private so nobody can see the financials. Step three: optimize for revenue. Step four: shield from accountability. It's a playbook. And it's being run on the infrastructure that decides whether you get to live somewhere.

And RealPage wasn't just scoring tenants. It was also allegedly fixing their rents. In August 2024, the Department of Justice and eight state Attorneys General filed an antitrust lawsuit alleging that RealPage's pricing algorithm — originally marketed as YieldStar, now rebranded as "AI Revenue Management" — enabled landlords to systematically coordinate rental prices. ProPublica had exposed in 2022 that the software told landlords to raise rents even if it meant leaving units vacant to drive up demand. RealPage's own marketing used to brag it was "the industry's only price optimization solution" that could "consistently reduce vacancies and maximize rents." The company even claimed the algorithm "helps eliminate the risk of collusion" — which is exactly what the DOJ says it was doing. By January 2025, the DOJ had added lawsuits against six of the largest landlords in the country. New Jersey's Attorney General called it "a rent-setting cartel." So the same company scoring whether you qualify for an apartment was also allegedly coordinating how much you'd pay for it. And it was all happening behind a wall of private equity secrecy.

But the housing scores are only one layer. Because the same machine is running in hiring.

---

Derek Mobley is a Black man over forty who lives in Northern California. Over the course of seven years, he applied to more than a hundred jobs through platforms that use Workday's AI screening system. He was rejected from every single one. Every. Single. One. He knows no human being ever read his applications because one night he submitted a resume at 12:55 AM and received a rejection at 1:50 AM. Less than an hour. On a weekend.

Derek sued Workday. And in May 2025, a judge granted conditional collective action certification, meaning the case could now potentially represent millions of applicants over forty who were screened by Workday's algorithm since September 2020. The court also ruled something that sent shockwaves through the industry: the AI company itself — not just the employer using the tool — can be directly liable for employment discrimination.

But Derek's story has company. Remember Kyle Behm. Kyle was a young man with bipolar disorder who applied for a job at Kroger in 2012. He was rejected after a personality test built by Kronos — a test that used clinical psychological screening questions. The kind of questions a psychiatrist would ask. Kyle was systematically filtered out because of his mental health. He kept applying. He kept getting rejected. His father Roland became an advocate, trying to fight these tests. Kyle died by suicide in 2019.

And the bias in these systems isn't anecdotal. Brookings studied AI resume screening and found that white-associated names were preferred in eighty-five point one percent of tests. Black-associated names? Eight point six percent. Equal treatment? Just six point three percent of the time. Eighty-two percent of companies now use AI to review resumes. So if you're wondering why you keep applying and never hearing back — it might not be your resume. It might be a number you can't see.

And it's not just resume screening. The EEOC settled its first-ever AI discrimination case in 2023 against iTutorGroup, a company that programmed its hiring software to automatically reject women over fifty-five and men over sixty. How did they get caught? An applicant submitted two identical applications that differed only in birth date. She only got an interview when she used a fake, younger date. Over two hundred qualified workers were thrown out by a line of code. The settlement? Three hundred and sixty-five thousand dollars. Split across all the victims. That's less than two thousand dollars per person who was denied a job because of their age.

There's a woman identified as D.K. in court filings — Indigenous and Deaf, pursuing a master's in data science, already employed at Intuit with high performance scores. She was encouraged by her own supervisor to apply for a promotion. To do so, she had to complete a HireVue video interview. Research shows automated speech recognition performs ten times worse for deaf individuals — roughly every other word gets transcribed incorrectly. D.K. requested human-generated captioning. Intuit said no. After the interview, she was rejected. The feedback? Work on "effective communication." Provide "concise and direct answers." Practice "active listening." Every single piece of feedback was a coded reference to her disability. The ACLU filed charges in March 2025.

Amazon built its own version of this internally. They created an AI system to rate job candidates on a one-to-five-star scale. Trained on ten years of resumes — which, because the tech industry skews male, were mostly from men. The algorithm learned to penalize resumes that contained the word "women's," as in "women's chess club captain." It downgraded graduates of all-women's colleges. It favored what Amazon engineers called "macho verbs" like "executed" and "captured." They scrapped the project by 2017. But here's the thing: Amazon had the resources and the self-awareness to catch the bias and kill the tool. Most companies using off-the-shelf hiring AI don't even know to look.

So the algorithms are failing in hiring. They're failing in housing. But there's a third arena where the scores are even more invisible.

---

LexisNexis Risk Solutions holds eighty-three billion public records on two hundred and eighty-two million unique identities. That's roughly two hundred and ninety records for every person in America. Their database runs on six petabytes of data, processes a hundred million events per day, and tracks one point four billion online identities.

And they acquired much of this power through a single deal: the three-point-six-billion-dollar purchase of ChoicePoint in 2008. That's how they got CLUE — the Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange. CLUE contains over two hundred and seventy million insurance claims events, with ninety-nine point six five percent of insurers contributing data. When you file an auto or home insurance claim, it goes into CLUE and stays there for seven years. And the next time you shop for insurance, every company you talk to can see it.

But CLUE is just the claims database. The real power is in the scores built on top of it. LexisNexis produces a Risk Classifier score from 200 to 997. It pulls from motor vehicle records, property records, court records, and credit data. If you opt into the version that includes medical data, it also factors in your prescription history and clinical lab reports. One of their own findings: your mortality risk drops by thirty-nine percent if you own property. So if you rent, the algorithm literally thinks you're more likely to die.

Then there's Verisk Analytics, which holds thirty-two billion standardized insurance records and has collected two hundred and sixty billion miles of driving behavior data from more than eight million vehicles through partnerships with Ford, GM, Honda, and Hyundai. Their DrivingDNA score claims a twelve-times difference in expected losses between the worst-scoring and best-scoring groups. They can calculate that score with as little as ten weeks of data from your car.

Your car is reporting on you to your insurance company, and your insurance company is scoring you based on what your car said. And you have no idea what the score says.

Now, you might think: I'm a good driver, I don't file claims, I have nothing to worry about. But the scores aren't just about your behavior. They're about where you live, what you buy, and who your neighbors are.

Credit-based insurance scores — used by ninety-five percent of home insurers and virtually all auto insurers — have a well-documented racial disparity. The FTC found that African Americans and Hispanics are heavily overrepresented in the lowest scoring categories. The Consumer Federation of America found that predominantly Black neighborhoods pay seventy percent more for auto insurance on average. A safe driver in Buffalo's predominantly Black zip code 14215 was quoted thirty-four percent higher premiums than an identical driver in the neighboring majority-white zip code 14226. Same driving record. Same car. Different neighborhood. Different price.

Carmen Arroyo knows what it's like to watch a score destroy a life. Her son Mikhail was severely injured in an accident in 2015 — left unable to speak, walk, or care for himself. Carmen asked her landlord for permission to move Mikhail into her home so she could take care of him. The landlord ran a background check through CoreLogic's CrimSAFE system. It came back with one notation: "disqualifying criminal record." The record was a retail theft charge in Pennsylvania for a hundred and fifty dollars. The charge had been dropped. Never resulted in a conviction. But the algorithm didn't note that. CoreLogic refused to give the Arroyos a copy of the actual criminal history. Mikhail was left in an institution for approximately a year while his mother fought the system.

A dismissed charge. A broken algorithm. A son left in a facility he didn't need to be in. Because a computer said no.

And it's not just housing and hiring where these invisible judgments cause real harm. In healthcare, a widely used algorithm built by Optum — a UnitedHealth Group subsidiary — was managing care decisions for roughly two hundred million patients across the country. Researchers at UC Berkeley discovered in 2019 that the algorithm was racially biased, not because anyone programmed racism into it, but because it predicted healthcare costs instead of illness. Since Black patients historically received less care and therefore spent less money, the algorithm concluded they were healthier. Among patients at the ninety-seventh percentile risk score, Black patients actually had twenty-six percent more chronic illnesses than white patients with the same score. One Cigna doctor — using a different AI system — denied sixty thousand claims in a single month without opening a single patient file. Nationally, insurers denied over forty-nine million claims in 2021. Patients appealed less than two-tenths of one percent of them. The algorithm says no, and people just accept it.

---

So is anyone actually doing something about this? The answer is: barely, and slowly.

New York City passed Local Law 144 in 2023, requiring companies to audit their hiring algorithms for bias and tell candidates when AI is being used. Sounds great on paper. But a December 2025 audit by the State Comptroller found that seventy-five percent of complaints about the law were misrouted and never reached the right agency. The agency surveyed thirty-two companies and found just one violation. The Comptroller's own auditors reviewed the same companies and found at least seventeen.

Illinois passed the strongest biometric privacy law in the country — BIPA — which lets individuals sue for up to five thousand dollars per violation when their biometric data is collected without consent. A class action against HireVue under BIPA is ongoing. Illinois also passed an AI hiring law effective January 2026 that bars discriminatory AI in recruiting and hiring.

Colorado passed the first comprehensive state AI Act, requiring impact assessments, consumer disclosure, and the right to appeal algorithmic decisions through human review. But enforcement was delayed to June 2026, and only the Attorney General can bring cases — no private right of action.

Minnesota banned screening algorithms that have a disproportionate effect on protected classes. Oregon now requires individualized assessment before anyone can be denied housing based on criminal history. Washington requires portable tenant screening reports valid for thirty days.

But here's the critical gap: there is no federal law governing algorithmic scoring. The Algorithmic Accountability Act has been introduced three separate times. It has never made it out of committee. Not once. Meanwhile, the EU passed a comprehensive AI Act in 2024 that explicitly bans social scoring and classifies hiring algorithms, insurance scoring, and creditworthiness assessments as high-risk systems requiring transparency, audits, and human oversight. Violations carry fines of up to thirty-five million euros or seven percent of global revenue.

In America? TransUnion paid fifteen million dollars for fabricating eviction records. That's less than half a percent of their four-point-two-billion-dollar annual revenue. RealPage paid three million for inaccurate criminal matching. SafeRent paid two-point-two-seven-five million in the Mary Louis case. These aren't penalties. They're operating costs.

---

So what can you actually do? More than you think — but you have to know where to look.

First, and most importantly: request your reports. You are legally entitled to a free copy from every consumer reporting agency once a year. SafeRent, TransUnion SmartMove, CoreLogic, RealPage — they all have to give you your file. LexisNexis has to show you what's in their database and in your CLUE insurance report. The CFPB publishes a list of more than forty consumer reporting companies with contact information. Most people have never requested a single one.

Second: if you're denied housing, a job, or insurance based on a report, the company must give you an adverse action notice naming the screening company, telling you what was in the report, and informing you of your right to dispute it. If they don't give you that notice, they're violating federal law. Keep every rejection letter. Document everything.

Third: dispute aggressively. Send disputes to both the screening company and the company that furnished the data. Do it in writing. Send it certified mail. Under the FCRA, they have thirty days to investigate, and if they can't verify the information, they must delete it.

Fourth: file complaints everywhere. The CFPB complaint portal is still operational even with reduced staff. The FTC collects complaints to identify enforcement patterns. Your state Attorney General's office handles consumer protection. HUD handles housing discrimination cases. The EEOC handles employment. Individual complaints may feel pointless, but the CFPB's twenty-six thousand tenant screening complaints helped trigger enforcement actions worth millions. Every complaint creates a public record that regulators, journalists, and lawyers can use. File with all of them. It costs you nothing but time.

And fifth: opt out of data broker databases. LexisNexis has an opt-out page. So does Acxiom, Spokeo, and dozens of others. It won't erase everything — your data stays in law enforcement databases and regulated products — but it's a start.

Here's what I want to leave you with, though. And it's the part of this story that I think matters most.

Every company building these scores markets them the same way. "Streamline decisions." "Reduce risk." "Say goodbye to manual tasks." "Confident, informed decisions." The framing is always the same: the algorithm is objective, the human is biased. But the evidence shows the exact opposite. The algorithms amplify the biases already embedded in the data. They take the racism in historical lending, the errors in court records, the inequality in healthcare spending, and they automate it. They scale it. They make it faster and cheaper and invisible.

LexisNexis holds two hundred and ninety records on every person in America. Acxiom tracks three thousand data points on two hundred and sixty million people. Verisk has cataloged two hundred and sixty billion miles of your driving. HireVue has recorded seventy million job interviews. And all of that data flows through a pipeline that goes from courts and public records, to aggregators like LexisNexis, to screening companies like SafeRent and RealPage, to the landlord or employer or insurer who makes the final call. Errors compound at every step. Data collected for one purpose — a dismissed charge, a medical bill, a claim you filed after a storm — gets repurposed to deny you something years later. And at no point in that pipeline does anyone ask for your permission, or even tell you it's happening.

There are scores attached to your name right now that are shaping your life. You didn't ask for them. You can't see them. But now you know they exist. And once you know, you can start pulling your reports, checking for errors, and demanding the transparency that these companies have spent billions of dollars trying to avoid.

Mary Louis fought her score and won. The settlement in her case barred SafeRent from issuing scores for voucher holders entirely. Any future scoring tool they build has to be validated by an independent third party. It took a class-action lawsuit and a statement of interest from the Department of Justice. It took years. But she proved that a single person pushing back against the machine can change the machine.

Because the real question isn't whether you have a secret score. You do. The question is what you're going to do about it.
