Last month, Marquita Davis lost her apartment. Not because she stopped paying rent. Because an algorithm decided she should pay more.

Marquita is a medical assistant in Dallas. Two kids, seven and eleven. She'd lived in the same unit for three years — good tenant, never missed a payment. Then her rent jumped thirty percent in a single year. Twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She moved her kids into her mother's living room.

Here's what Marquita didn't know. Her landlord didn't raise her rent. A company called RealPage did. RealPage built software that takes confidential pricing data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing — and uses it to coordinate rent increases across more than ten million apartments nationwide. Ninety percent of landlords followed the algorithm's price. The Department of Justice called it price fixing. The settlement? Zero dollars. No fine. No compensation. No admission of guilt.

A company generating one point six billion in revenue walked away free. The algorithm is still running.

But this isn't about one company. This is about a formula — invented in 1977, perfected across five decades, and reaching a version that might be impossible to reverse.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula starts with a car on fire.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto had a design flaw. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. People burned to death. Ford's engineers found the flaw before the car shipped. Someone in the boardroom did the math. Fixing every Pinto: one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. Expected wrongful death lawsuits: forty-nine point five million. The memo recommended against the fix.

Families sued. Payouts totaled less than the fix. Indiana tried Ford for reckless homicide — one of the only times a corporation faced that charge. Acquitted. The Pinto was recalled, but the lesson had already landed. If the fine is less than the profit, proceed.

Since 2000, that lesson has generated more than one trillion dollars in corporate penalties across the United States. A hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid over a billion. They're all still operating.

For forty years, executives assumed there was a cost the spreadsheet couldn't capture — public shame. The Pinto became a cautionary tale. Nobody wanted to be the next Ford.

Marquita Davis wasn't born yet. But the formula that would raise her rent was already written. It just needed someone to prove that at sufficient scale, the math overwhelms the shame.

You have a Facebook account. Or you had one. Or someone made one for you in 2008 and you forgot about it. It doesn't matter. Your data was in the harvest.

In 2012, Facebook signed a consent decree with the Federal Trade Commission — essentially parole for corporations. A binding promise: stop misusing people's data. They signed it. Then they broke it.

Here's what actually happened. A Cambridge University researcher built a personality quiz app. Two hundred and seventy thousand people took it. Normal enough. But Facebook's platform allowed that app to also harvest the data of every friend connected to those quiz-takers — without those friends knowing, without them consenting, without them doing anything at all. From two hundred and seventy thousand users, the app reached eighty-seven million. Names, locations, likes, friend lists, messages, photos. Everything you'd ever given Facebook, packaged and sold to a company called Cambridge Analytica.

And here's what Cambridge Analytica did with it. They built psychographic profiles — personality maps of individual voters — and used them to target political ads designed to exploit specific emotional vulnerabilities. Anxious about immigration? You saw immigration ads. Angry about trade? Trade ads. Not based on what you searched for. Based on who you are — your personality, reverse-engineered from your likes and your friends' likes, turned into a weapon aimed at how you vote. Eighty-seven million people. People exactly like Marquita — same age, same city, same browsing habits. None of them knew.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission fined Facebook five billion dollars — the largest privacy penalty in history. Congress held hearings. The editorial boards called it a turning point.

The same day the fine was announced, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up. The stock gained seven billion in market cap — the punishment made Facebook two billion dollars richer than if the government had done nothing.

I need you to sit with that. The United States government announced the largest fine it had ever issued against a technology company. And the company's stock price rose. Five billion dollars was nine percent of one year's revenue. Wall Street did the math — the same math Ford did in 1977 — and concluded it was a bargain. Your data, your personality, your political vulnerabilities — all of it bought and sold, and the total cost to the company that enabled it made them more valuable.

Here's a thought experiment. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally immune for a year? Would you speed everywhere? Skip your taxes? Park wherever you want? The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing. And that's exactly what Facebook concluded — except they didn't ask you first. They ran the numbers, liked what they saw, and your data was the product.

The formula didn't just survive the front page. The front page made it stronger. And if you're watching this video, your data was almost certainly in that harvest. Not hypothetically. Actually.

So — Meta proved that the formula survives public shame. And every company watching learned the lesson. Not from a textbook. From a stock ticker. The ceiling had been removed. The only question left was whether the formula survives something shame never had to face: actual dead bodies.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. A hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 went down six minutes after takeoff. A hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane — the 737 MAX. Same flaw — a system called MCAS, the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System. A single sensor measured the nose angle. If it failed, the system shoved the nose toward the ground. Pilots couldn't override it.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four, heading to Kenya to work in global health. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who created modern consumer safety law. If you don't know that name: Ralph Nader is the reason your car has seatbelts and airbags. His work led to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the Consumer Product Safety Commission — the entire regulatory architecture designed to make corporate negligence impossible. Samya grew up in the family that built the safety system. The safety system failed to save her.

Boeing knew. Internal messages: "designed by clowns." Fixing MCAS properly meant re-certification — having the FAA verify the plane from scratch. Cost: up to a billion dollars. Boeing hid the flaw and shipped the plane.

The DOJ settled for two and a half billion. Victim fund: five hundred million split among three hundred and forty-six families — about one point four million each. Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. The fine was sixteen days of income. No executive went to prison. Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still got government contracts. Stock recovered.

Meta proved the formula survives shame. Boeing proved it survives death. Ralph Nader spent sixty years building the legal architecture designed to make this impossible — and the formula walked right through it.

And here's the strange thing. After Boeing, the formula should have been finished. Three hundred and forty-six dead. The regulatory system built by Ralph Nader himself couldn't stop it. If this were a movie, this would be the darkest moment — the part where you think the villain might actually win.

But the formula wasn't finished. It was evolving. And the next version was the one that scared me most — not because it was crueler, but because it was quieter.

The formula needs a villain. Right? Every case so far has had one. Ford had a memo with signatures. Boeing had engineers who hid a flaw. Meta had executives who allowed the harvest. You can point at someone and say: you.

What if the formula doesn't need a villain?

A bank — I won't name it yet — set sales quotas. Eight financial products per customer. Nearly impossible to hit honestly. But the quotas were tied to your job.

A branch manager in Florida. Bottom quartile in the weekly rankings. Her district manager calls. He doesn't say cheat. He says branches that underperform get restructured. So a teller opens a savings account for a customer who came in to deposit a check. The customer doesn't know. The teller doesn't feel great about it. But he watched two coworkers get fired last month. He hits his target. The branch moves up.

Multiply by five thousand three hundred employees. Three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. A retired teacher named Gloria found out she had one when a debt collector called.

The bank was Wells Fargo. And here's why this case matters more than it looks. There was no memo. No villain. No conspiracy. The quota system was the engine. Each manager was rational — push numbers or lose your job. Each teller was rational — fabricate or get replaced. Each executive was rational — revenue looks great, don't ask how. Nobody decided to create three point five million fake accounts. The system decided.

You can punish a person. You can't punish a system.

Three billion in fines. Revenue: eighty billion. Two weeks of income. Five thousand three hundred employees at the bottom were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits. And Marquita Davis? She's living inside a Wells Fargo right now. The algorithm that raised her rent doesn't have a name, a conscience, or a phone number. Nobody chose. Nobody's responsible.

Ford proved the fine is cheaper than the fix. Meta proved the formula survives shame. Boeing proved it survives death. Wells Fargo proved it doesn't need a villain. Each company watched the one before and drew the obvious conclusion.

The Sackler family drew a different one. What if you move the money before they even calculate the fine?

In Kermit, West Virginia — population four hundred — a single pharmacy received twelve million hydrocodone pills in three years. Twelve million. For four hundred people. Debra Vance worked at the Dollar General down the road. She started on OxyContin after a back injury in 2003 — prescribed, legal, doctor-approved. By 2008, she was buying pills from three different sources. She died in 2009. She was fifty-one. Nobody was charged.

Internal documents prove the Sacklers knew OxyContin was highly addictive. Instead of pulling back, they hired physicians to tell other doctors the addiction fears were overblown. More than six hundred thousand Americans have died from opioid overdoses. Before Purdue declared bankruptcy, the Sacklers systematically transferred billions in personal wealth out of the company. They saw the lawsuits coming. They moved the money first. The 2025 settlement: seven point four billion — structured to preserve most of a fortune between eleven and thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison.

Twelve thousand dollars per death. Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand in 1977. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper. Per death. Per decade. Per version.

The formula doesn't care which industry it runs in. Rent, data, planes, pills. It just needs numbers large enough that the math works.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

I almost stopped here. This would be a perfectly good video if it ended with a chart showing fines versus revenue across fifty years. But then I found the case that actually kept me up at night — not because it was the biggest, but because it was the one where you can't undo the damage. Even if you want to. Even if a court orders you to.

Every case so far has one structural feature in common. The violation came first. The fine came second. And in every case, the harm was — at least in theory — reversible. Ford could recall the car. Meta could delete the data. Boeing could ground the fleet. There was always an undo button. Expensive, slow, and usually nobody pressed it — but it existed.

What if it didn't?

In October 2025, a Reddit engineer did something clever. They created a post — an ordinary-looking post with a specific claim about a fictional company — and made it visible only through Google's search index. You couldn't find it by browsing Reddit. You couldn't stumble on it. The only way to reach it was through a search engine crawler. It was bait. A tripwire designed to catch one thing: an AI scraping Reddit's content without permission.

Within hours, Perplexity AI cited the post. Word for word. The trap worked.

Reddit's legal team sent a cease-and-desist letter. Standard procedure — stop scraping our content, or we'll sue. What happened next is the part that should concern you. Perplexity's citations to Reddit content didn't go down. They went up. Forty times. They scraped more after being told to stop. Not a little more. Forty times more.

Think about what that means. A company received a formal legal demand to stop taking content that wasn't theirs. Their response was to take forty times as much. Because they'd done the math. The data was worth more than any fine. The model being trained on that data was worth more than any lawsuit. And once the data is inside the model, there's no retrieving it.

That's why this is different from every other case in this video. With Ford, you recall the car. With Meta, you delete the data. With Boeing, you ground the fleet. With Purdue, you pull the pills. Each violation had an off switch. But with AI, the violation is the product. Scraped data is woven into the model during training, like sugar dissolved in water. You can't un-dissolve it. Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models.

The fine doesn't punish the theft. It becomes the purchase price — paid after the fact — for data already consumed.

And this isn't abstract. If you've ever posted on Reddit, commented on YouTube, written a blog post, tweeted, left a review — your words are in training datasets right now. Not hypothetically. Provably. Common Crawl, the dataset most large language models are trained on, contains over two hundred and fifty billion web pages. Your content is in there. And the companies using it to build hundred-billion-dollar products did not ask your permission. By the time the lawsuits settle — years from now — the models will already be built, deployed, and generating revenue. Your words, your ideas, your writing style — purchased at whatever price the court eventually sets. Which, if fifty years of data tells us anything, will be less than the profit.

Ninety active lawsuits as of March 2026. Every major AI company is being sued. Anthropic settled for one point five billion — two point five percent of their valuation. The model kept running the next day. OpenAI raised six point six billion in new funding while being sued by the New York Times. The investors didn't blink. The lawsuits aren't obstacles. They're overhead.

A children's book author named Sarah spent twelve years building a career. Seven published books. School visits. A modest but real living. Then a reader sent her a screenshot — a chatbot had generated a story in her exact style. Her sentence rhythm, her paragraph structure, the voice she'd spent a decade developing. All seven books scraped. She joined a class action. Her legal fees will likely exceed her share of any settlement. The model that absorbed her voice is running right now — and the formula says her lawsuit, if she wins, will cost less than the value of what was taken.

So is the formula unbeatable?

No. Because someone already broke it.

The European Union passed GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — and it did one thing differently. Fines scale to four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta didn't fight it. Within weeks, they rewrote their data transfer agreements, shut down a cross-border data pipeline they'd operated for a decade, and rebuilt their European infrastructure from scratch. The same company that shrugged off a five-billion-dollar American fine moved heaven and earth over a European penalty — because four percent of global revenue made the violation more expensive than the profit for the first time.

Proportional fines. Not a negotiated number — a percentage of everything. Criminal liability for the people who sign off, not the people at the bottom. And disgorgement — forcing a company to hand back every dollar earned from the violation. Not pay a fine on top. Return it.

So why hasn't the United States done the same thing? Because the companies that benefit from the formula are the same companies that fund the campaigns, staff the advisory boards, and lobby the committees that write the rules. Every attempt to import proportional fines has died before reaching a vote. The math won't change because the people who profit from the math are the ones who control the legislation.

Marquita Davis is still in her mother's living room. Her kids still share a room. The algorithm that raised her rent is still running. The company that built it paid nothing.

Ford. Meta. Boeing. Wells Fargo. Purdue. Every AI company training on your data right now. They all ran the same equation. Fine minus profit. And every time, the answer was the same.

If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class.
