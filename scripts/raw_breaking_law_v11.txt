Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across ten million apartments. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords and told every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company making one point six billion a year walked away without paying a cent. The algorithm is still running. Your rent is still high. And nobody seems to think this is strange.

But it is strange. So I started asking: how? What system produces that result? And what I found is a formula — elegant, brutal, and almost fifty years old — that explains not just RealPage, but everything. Every major corporate scandal you've ever heard of runs on the same math: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how it evolved, and why the newest version — happening right now — might be the one we can't undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula starts in 1977. With a memo that should have killed a company.

The Ford Pinto was a cheap compact car with a fuel tank that ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. Ford's engineers knew before the car shipped. Now — imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers say the fix costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. But the lawsuits from letting people burn to death? Only forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended the forty-nine million.

This was a real document, presented in a real courtroom. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number they pulled from the government's own safety guidelines. They used Washington's math to justify letting their customers burn.

And it worked exactly as predicted. People died. Families sued. But the settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal.

In 1977, this was front-page scandal. Genuinely shocking. So you'd expect the system to have shut this down. But it didn't. Because the lesson corporate America learned wasn't "don't kill people." It was: the formula works. And that raised a dangerous question — if you can get away with it when people die, what can't you get away with?

Forty-two years later, someone tested that question. And the answer was breathtaking.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission levied the largest privacy fine in history: five billion dollars. The target: Meta, then called Facebook. Here's what happened. In 2012, Facebook signed a consent decree — that's essentially a parole deal with the government, a legally binding promise to stop deceiving users about how their data was shared. Facebook broke it. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica — a company that harvested personal profiles to build voter manipulation tools — paid for access to eighty-seven million users' data without their knowledge. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock went up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. If you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket for something that made you billions. But the real punchline is bigger. The stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing at all.

Somewhere in Washington, a regulator was writing a press release about accountability. On Wall Street? Champagne. You genuinely can't write satire this good.

Now — Ford proved the formula works, but Ford was at least punished reputationally. People were outraged. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. So Meta raised the stakes: what if you use the formula and nobody even cares? What if the market applauds you for getting caught? Meta didn't just survive punishment. It profited from it. And every company in America noticed.

Which raises the next question — one that should genuinely scare you. The formula survived money. It survived privacy violations. It survived public outrage. But does it survive death? Is there a moral cost high enough to break the math?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that question.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — one hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

Boeing knew. Their engineers flagged the problem before certification. Internal messages called it a plane "designed by clowns, supervised by monkeys." But here's the calculation: fixing it meant re-certification — the process where federal regulators independently verify a plane is safe to fly. Cost: five hundred million to one billion. So instead of going back to the FAA, Boeing hid the flaw. They shipped the plane. MCAS killed one hundred and eighty-nine people, and because the fix was never made, killed one hundred and fifty-seven more five months later.

Settlement: two and a half billion. Mostly paid to airlines, not families. No executive imprisoned. Boeing's revenue: fifty-eight billion a year. Three hundred and forty-six deaths cost them sixteen days of income.

Then Boeing violated the settlement. They broke the deal about the law they broke that killed those people. Still making planes. Still getting contracts. Stock recovered.

The formula held. Death didn't break it.

And you know what? After all this — after the Pinto memo, after two planes fell out of the sky, after the five-billion-dollar fine that made Facebook richer — do you know what the system changed? The press releases got longer. The FTC started using the word "historic" more often. Congress held hearings where senators asked tough questions and then went to lunch. The whole regulatory apparatus developed this elaborate performance of accountability — serious faces, big numbers, stern language — that looks exactly like justice and does absolutely nothing. It's theater. Very expensive theater, produced at a cost of one trillion dollars, with a standing ovation from the stock market every single time.

I almost admire it. Almost.

But the formula was still, in each case, a decision. One company. One boardroom. Someone chose. So — different question. What happens when nobody chooses? When the formula becomes so embedded that five thousand three hundred people commit fraud simultaneously, and not one of them thinks they're the problem?

Between 2002 and 2016, five thousand three hundred Wells Fargo employees created three point five million fake accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without consent. Not rogues — five thousand three hundred people, because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. The pressure was so intense that fraud was the only way to keep your job. Management built a machine, and fraud was the output.

Five thousand three hundred workers were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits. Three billion in fines. Eighty billion in revenue. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud.

Ford created the formula. Meta proved the market rewards it. Boeing proved death doesn't break it. Wells Fargo proved it can run on autopilot. But none of those cases answer the darkest version of the question: what happens when the formula is applied to a product that kills six hundred thousand people — and the people who ran it get to keep the money?

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. They knew OxyContin was highly addictive. Internal documents prove they knew people were dying. They pushed higher doses. They made billions. More than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses — more than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the Sacklers agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth: eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin on a product that killed six hundred thousand people. In 1977, Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand dollars. The Sacklers paid about twelve thousand per death. The formula didn't just survive. It got cheaper.

Five companies. Fifty years. In every case, the fine was a fraction of the profit — from zero percent to twenty-one percent, and even the highest took six hundred thousand deaths and fifty states to achieve. One trillion dollars in corporate fines since 2000. A hundred and twenty-seven companies paying over a billion each, repeatedly. Repeat offenders get smaller fines, not larger.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Now — every case I've shown you has one thing in common. The violation happened first. The fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta collected the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid. But what if an entire industry studied this fifty-year playbook, did the math upfront, and decided to break the law as a business strategy — before the first product shipped?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

Ninety active copyright lawsuits as of March 2026. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company, sued for scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, training models on content they never paid for.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a newspaper from Claude. Even if every lawsuit succeeds, the models survive. The fine becomes the purchase price for stolen data.

Anthropic settled for one point five billion in September 2025 after authors proved they trained on millions of pirated books. Valuation: sixty billion. Settlement: two point five percent. The model? Still running.

OpenAI: the New York Times sued for billions. A judge ordered twenty million ChatGPT logs turned over. While the courts crawl, OpenAI raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

Perplexity: Reddit set a trap — a hidden post only Google could find. Perplexity surfaced it within hours. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's citations increased forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the cost of raw material taken without permission.

And the next generation — training now — is trained on you. Your conversations. Your emails. Your photos. Your voice. When the lawsuits come, the models will already be built.

"Move fast and break things" never meant software. It meant the law.

Does it have to be this way? No. Someone already proved it doesn't.

The EU's GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, Meta changed how they operate across all of Europe. Not because they chose to. Because the math changed.

Proportional fines work. Criminal liability works — executives face prison, not just corporate checks. Disgorgement works — you return every dollar. Structural remedies work — breakups, enforceable decrees, industry bans.

If the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. If executives face prison, the decision changes. None of this is radical. It's just math.

But until the math changes, every company will run the same formula Ford wrote in 1977 — the one Meta validated, Boeing tested with human lives, Wells Fargo industrialized, the Sacklers pushed to its extreme, and every AI company is perfecting right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Pinto memo is forty-nine years old. The formula has been perfected, not eliminated. But the question at its heart hasn't changed: will the fine be less than the profit?

The only thing that breaks it is math. Not outrage. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop — not because they find their conscience, but because they find a new spreadsheet.

We know what works. The question is why we haven't done it.

Now you know the math.
