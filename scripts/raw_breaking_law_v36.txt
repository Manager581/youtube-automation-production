Last month, Marquita Davis lost her apartment. Not because she stopped paying rent. Because an algorithm decided she should pay more.

Marquita is a medical assistant in Dallas. Two kids, seven and eleven. She'd lived in the same unit for three years — good tenant, never missed a payment. Then her rent jumped thirty percent in a single year. Twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She moved her kids into her mother's living room.

Here's what Marquita didn't know. Her landlord didn't raise her rent. A company called RealPage did. RealPage built software that takes confidential pricing data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing — and uses it to coordinate rent increases across more than ten million apartments nationwide. Ninety percent of landlords followed the algorithm's price. The Department of Justice called it price fixing. The settlement? Zero dollars. No fine. No compensation. No admission of guilt.

A company generating one point six billion in revenue walked away free. The algorithm is still running.

But this isn't about one company. This is about a formula — invented in 1977, perfected across five decades, and reaching a version that might be impossible to reverse.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula starts with a car on fire.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto had a design flaw. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. People burned to death. Ford's engineers found the flaw before the car shipped. Someone in the boardroom did the math. Fixing every Pinto: one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. Expected wrongful death lawsuits: forty-nine point five million. The memo recommended against the fix.

Families sued. Payouts totaled less than the fix. Indiana tried Ford for reckless homicide — one of the only times a corporation faced that charge. Acquitted. The Pinto was recalled, but the lesson had already landed. If the fine is less than the profit, proceed.

Since 2000, that lesson has generated more than one trillion dollars in corporate penalties across the United States. A hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid over a billion. They're all still operating.

For decades after Ford, the formula had one weakness: shame. The Pinto was front-page news. Congressional hearings. Recall campaigns. The name became a punchline — nobody wanted to be the next Ford. Every executive in America understood there was a cost the spreadsheet couldn't capture. Public outrage. Reputational destruction. You might survive the fine. You would never survive the front page.

This kept the formula in check for forty years. Not because executives became moral, but because the shame made the math uncertain. The fine was predictable — a number on a spreadsheet. The public backlash wasn't. And uncertainty is the one thing the formula can't handle.

Marquita Davis wasn't born yet. But the formula that would raise her rent was already written. It just needed someone to prove the shame didn't work anymore.

In 2019, someone did.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission fined Facebook five billion dollars — the largest privacy penalty in history. Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012, which is essentially parole for corporations — a binding promise to stop misusing data. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica harvested eighty-seven million users' personal data to build voter manipulation tools. Facebook allowed it.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic. Congress held hearings. The editorial boards called it a turning point.

The same day the fine was announced, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up. The stock gained seven billion in market cap — the punishment made Facebook two billion dollars richer than if the government had done nothing.

I want to stay on that for a second. Because the absurdity of it deserves its own moment. The United States government announced the largest fine it had ever issued against a technology company. The company's response was to become more valuable. The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. Wall Street did the math — the same math Ford did in 1977 — and concluded it was a bargain. The formula didn't just survive the front page. The front page made it stronger.

Eighty-seven million profiles. Statistically, people exactly like Marquita — same age, same city, same browsing habits — were in that harvest. None of them knew.

Now — because Meta proved the formula survives public shame, every company watching knew the ceiling had been removed. The shame tax was gone. The only question left was whether the formula would survive something shame never had to face: actual dead bodies. Because if you can kill three hundred and forty-six people and the math still works, there is nothing left to stop it.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. A hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 went down six minutes after takeoff. A hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane — the 737 MAX. Same flaw — a system called MCAS, the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System. A single sensor measured the nose angle. If it failed, the system shoved the nose toward the ground. Pilots couldn't override it.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four, heading to Kenya to work in global health. I want to tell you about her family, because it matters. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader. If you don't know that name — Ralph Nader is the reason your car has seatbelts, airbags, and a collapsible steering column. He wrote a book in 1965 called Unsafe at Any Speed that created the modern consumer safety movement. His work led to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration, the Consumer Product Safety Commission, the regulatory architecture that was supposed to make corporate negligence impossible in America. Samya Stumo grew up in the family that built the safety system. And the safety system failed to save her.

In court, Samya's family proved Boeing knew about the flaw. Internal messages: "designed by clowns." Fixing MCAS properly meant re-certification — having the FAA verify the plane from scratch. Cost: up to a billion dollars. Boeing hid the flaw and shipped the plane.

The DOJ settled for two and a half billion. Victim fund: five hundred million split among three hundred and forty-six families — about one point four million each. Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. The fine was sixteen days of income. No executive went to prison. Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still got government contracts. Stock recovered.

Meta proved the formula survives shame. Boeing proved it survives death. Ralph Nader spent sixty years building a legal system to protect people like Samya — and the formula walked right through it. So if shame can't stop it, and death can't stop it, and the entire regulatory apparatus built by the most famous consumer advocate in American history can't stop it — what happens when someone scales the body count by a factor of two thousand?

In Kermit, West Virginia — population four hundred — a single pharmacy received twelve million hydrocodone pills in three years. Twelve million. For four hundred people. That's thirty thousand pills per resident.

The Sackler family owned Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. Instead of pulling back, they hired physicians to tell other doctors the addiction fears were overblown. Dr. Russell Portenoy spent years making that case at medical conferences. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." More than six hundred thousand Americans have died from opioid overdoses.

Before Purdue declared bankruptcy, the Sacklers systematically transferred billions in personal wealth out of the company. They saw the lawsuits coming. They moved the money first. The 2025 settlement: seven point four billion — structured to preserve most of a fortune that sits between eleven and thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison.

Twelve thousand dollars per death. Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand in 1977. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper. Per death. Per decade. Per version.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

I almost stopped here. This would be a perfectly good video if it ended with a chart showing fines versus revenue across fifty years. But then I found the case that actually kept me up at night — not because it was the biggest, but because it was the one where the formula stopped needing a human to run it.

Every case so far — Ford, Meta, Boeing, Purdue — has one thing in common. Someone chose. A specific person made a specific decision, and you can point at them and say: you. Which means someone could have chosen differently.

The formula needs a villain. Right?

What if it doesn't?

This is the part that changed how I think about all of this. Because everything up to now has a face you can blame.

Wells Fargo set sales quotas — eight financial products per customer. Checking, savings, credit cards, home equity, insurance. Nearly impossible to hit honestly. But the quotas were tied to your job.

Start with a branch manager in Florida — call her Sandra. Twelve employees. Corporate sends weekly rankings. Sandra's branch is in the bottom quartile. Her district manager calls. He doesn't say cheat. He says branches that underperform get restructured.

A teller named Marcus opens a savings account for a customer who came in to deposit a check. The customer doesn't know. Marcus doesn't feel great about it. But he watched two coworkers get fired last month, and he has student loans. The account generates a small fee. Marcus hits his target. Sandra's branch moves up. The district manager doesn't call.

Multiply by five thousand three hundred employees. Over several years, they created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without consent. A retired teacher named Gloria found out she had a Wells Fargo credit card when a debt collector called.

Here's what makes Wells Fargo different from everything else in this video. At Ford, someone wrote a memo. At Boeing, someone hid a flaw. At Purdue, someone moved the money. Those are decisions by identifiable people. At Wells Fargo, there was no memo. No villain. The quota system was the engine. Each branch manager was rational — push numbers or lose your job. Each teller was rational — fabricate or get replaced. Each executive was rational — revenue looks great, don't ask how.

Nobody decided to create three point five million fake accounts. The system decided. And you can punish a person. You can't punish a system.

Think about what that means for every industry. You don't need a conspiracy. You don't even need bad intentions. You just need a set of incentives where cheating is the rational choice at every level of the organization. The fraud emerges on its own, like mold in a warm room. And when regulators finally notice — years later, always years later — there's nobody to arrest. Because everybody was just doing their job.

Wells Fargo paid three billion in fines. Revenue: eighty billion. Two weeks of income. Five thousand three hundred employees at the bottom were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

The Ford Pinto had a villain — a memo with names. Wells Fargo didn't need one. The system was the villain. And that's the version of the formula that's hardest to stop — because you can't fine a system into behaving.

Now. I need you to hold everything I've just told you in your head — Ford, Meta, Boeing, Purdue, Wells Fargo — because what comes next is the reason I made this video. Every case so far shares one structural feature. And it's the feature that's about to disappear.

The violation happened first. The fine came second. Ford could recall the car. Meta could delete the data. Boeing could ground the fleet. Purdue could pull the pills. Even Wells Fargo could close the fake accounts. The harm was always — at least in theory — reversible. There was an undo button. It was expensive and slow and usually nobody pressed it, but it existed.

What if it didn't? What if the violation and the product were the same thing — and you couldn't return the stolen goods even if a court ordered you to?

In October 2025, Reddit set a trap. They created a post visible only through Google indexing and waited. Perplexity AI scraped it. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's citations to Reddit content went up forty times. They scraped more after being told to stop.

That single incident tells you where the formula has arrived. Ninety active lawsuits as of March 2026. Every major AI company is being sued for training models on data they never had permission to use — books, journalism, music, code, personal conversations.

Here's why this is different from everything before. With Ford, you recall the car. With Meta, you delete the data. With Boeing, you ground the fleet. With Purdue, you pull the pills. Each violation had an off switch. But with AI, the violation is the product. Scraped data is woven into the model during training, like sugar dissolved in water. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a stolen book from Claude. Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models.

The fine doesn't punish the theft. It becomes the purchase price — paid after the fact — for data already consumed.

Every AI company studied fifty years of corporate penalties and arrived at the same conclusion — not accidentally, but as a deliberate strategy. Take everything. Build fast. Settle later. The formula's final form isn't a bug. It's the business model.

A novelist named Sarah discovered her entire backlist — seven books, a decade of work — had been ingested by a language model when a reader showed her that a chatbot could reproduce her prose style on command. Not quotes. Her style. The rhythm of her sentences, the way she built paragraphs, the voice she'd spent years developing. She joined a class action with thousands of other writers. The settlement, if it comes, will be pennies per author. The model that learned her voice will keep running.

Anthropic settled for one point five billion in September 2025 — two point five percent of their valuation. The model kept running the next day. OpenAI raised six point six billion in new funding while actively being sued by the New York Times. A judge ordered them to hand over twenty million ChatGPT logs. The investors didn't blink. The lawsuits aren't obstacles. They're overhead.

And the next generation of models is being trained on something more personal than books. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails processed by AI assistants. Your voice, fed into transcription services. When those lawsuits come — and they will — the models will already be built. The purchase will already be complete.

"Move fast and break things" was never about breaking software. It was always about breaking the law. And this time, unlike Ford, unlike Meta, unlike Boeing — it can't be undone.

Right now, Marquita Davis is using a customer service chatbot to dispute her rent. That conversation is training data for the next model. That model will optimize pricing for the next generation of rental algorithms. The fine will come later. The model will already exist. The rent will already be set. The formula keeps running.

So is it unbeatable?

No. Because someone already broke it.

The European Union passed GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — and it did one thing differently. Fines scale to four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta didn't fight it, didn't lobby, didn't wait. Within weeks, they rewrote their data transfer agreements, shut down a cross-border data pipeline they'd operated for a decade, and rebuilt their European infrastructure from scratch. The same company that shrugged off a five-billion-dollar FTC fine moved heaven and earth over a European penalty — because four percent of global revenue made the violation more expensive than the profit for the first time.

Three things. Proportional fines — scaled to global revenue, not negotiated down to a fraction. Criminal liability for the executives who sign off — not the five thousand three hundred employees at the bottom. And disgorgement — the legal term for forcing a company to return every dollar earned from the violation. Not a fine on top. Hand it back. All of it.

The fix exists. It works. It's been proven in the world's largest single market. So why hasn't the United States done the same thing?

Because the formula has lobbyists. The ten most penalized US corporations spend hundreds of millions per year on lobbying — more than many countries spend on their entire justice systems. Every attempt to bring GDPR-style enforcement to the US has died in committee, killed by the same companies the law would constrain. The math won't change because the people who benefit from the math are the ones writing the rules.

Repeat offenders — companies caught breaking the law multiple times — receive smaller fines as a percentage of revenue than first-time offenders. The system doesn't just fail to punish recidivism. It rewards it.

Marquita Davis is still in her mother's living room. Her kids still share a room. The algorithm that raised her rent is still running. RealPage paid nothing. And somewhere right now, an AI model is training on her data to build the next version of the system that displaced her family.

If the penalty for a crime is a fine, the law only exists for the lower class. That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
