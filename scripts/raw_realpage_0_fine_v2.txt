In late 2023, investigators in the Phoenix Attorney General's office noticed something.

Rents in the area were up seventy-six percent in seven years.

Four apartment buildings — different owners, different operators — had landed within twelve percent of each other on price. Not close in some categories. Close in all of them.

The buildings were supposed to be competing with each other. They were acting like the same landlord.

Because, in a sense, they were.

# ── ACT 1 — WHAT THE ALGORITHM DID (0:45-3:00) ─────────────────────────────
# NO thesis paragraph. Viewer figures out what the video is about by watching.

YieldStar is a revenue management platform. Landlords pay a subscription, hand over their rent rolls, their occupancy data, their move-outs, their renewals — everything they know about the units they own. In return, YieldStar tells them what to charge. Unit by unit. Day by day.

On its own, that is nothing new. Hotels have priced rooms this way for thirty years. Airlines do the same thing with seats.

But apartments are not seats. You do not choose to rent an apartment tonight because Wednesdays are off-peak. You rent because your current lease is ending, your job is here, your kid's school is here, and moving costs an average of twelve hundred dollars even if nothing breaks.

A renter isn't a shopper. A renter signs what you put in front of them.

[BEAT]

What RealPage added to the formula was the thing that made it illegal.

The algorithm did not just price one landlord's units. It priced across landlords. To use YieldStar, you had to share your own data with the pool. Your rents, your specials, your vacancies — all of it, fed into a single model that then told you, and your competitors, what everyone should charge.

If you were a single landlord with a YieldStar subscription, you were not making a pricing decision. You were accepting one.

If you were in a market where every major landlord used it — and by 2022, in some cities, that was most of them — the market was no longer a market. It was a shared spreadsheet.

Price fixing is not a complicated crime. Two companies that are supposed to compete agree, directly or indirectly, to set prices together. It has been illegal in the United States since the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890.

The prosecutor investigating RealPage did not have to invent a new category of harm. He had to prove the old one was still happening.

# ── ACT 2 — THE MATH (3:00-5:30) ───────────────────────────────────────────

Now the math.

[VERIFY BEFORE NARRATION: ProPublica footprint estimate, rent-premium
 range studies, metro-area concentrations. Ranges below are well-reported
 but confirm current numbers.]

RealPage has been in the rental pricing business since the early 2000s. By the early 2020s, independent investigations — most notably by ProPublica — estimated that YieldStar or its sister products priced somewhere between sixteen and twenty percent of all large multi-family apartments in the United States.

In certain metro areas, the concentration was much higher. In Seattle, some neighborhoods pushed past seventy percent. In Denver, similar. In parts of Phoenix — where the attorney general's office first flagged the pattern — the number approached eighty percent.

Translate that into rent.

Economists looking at YieldStar-saturated markets have consistently found a rent premium between three and nine percent, relative to comparable non-algorithmic markets.

Take the midpoint. Five percent.

On ten million apartments — the most widely cited figure for YieldStar's footprint at peak — a five percent rent premium is, conservatively, billions of dollars a year.

Not across a decade. Every year.

[VISUAL: simple chart. The rent-premium bar, set against the fine bar.]

[BEAT]

This is the number that matters.

Because on one side of this chart is what the algorithm extracted from renters — measured in billions per year, year after year. On the other side is what the company behind the algorithm paid for the privilege.

Whatever that second number is, it is not a deterrent. It is a receipt.

Here is the part most of the coverage hasn't caught up to.

The Department of Justice filed its antitrust case against RealPage in August 2024. Multiple state attorneys general joined it. Private renters added more than thirty separate lawsuits on top. And yet, as of today —

[VISUAL: a server rack, humming. A product page. A login screen.]

The algorithm is still running.

Landlords are still subscribing. Prices are still being set. RealPage has renamed the product, adjusted some of its disclosures, and denied the central allegation. The case grinds on. And somewhere, in four buildings in Phoenix with different owners and identical rents, another rental cycle starts next month.

[BEAT]

A handful of jurisdictions have tried something real.

[VERIFY BEFORE NARRATION: Seattle algorithmic rent-setting ban was proposed
 in 2024; confirm whether it passed or remains pending. If it passed, cite.
 If still pending, use softer language ("has considered"). San Francisco
 passed a similar measure; verify. Also: Minneapolis, Philadelphia — check.]

Seattle considered banning the use of algorithmic pricing tools on residential rentals. San Francisco moved in the same direction. The proposals targeted the specific mechanism — data-pooling across landlords — rather than the broader antitrust question. They would not undo the harm. But they would, at least, stop the machine.

Whether any of those measures survives, whether the federal case ends in a settlement worth anything close to what the algorithm extracted, whether the company pays a number large enough to change the next quarterly meeting about the next algorithm — none of that is decided yet.

[BEAT]

What is decided is this.

[VISUAL: return to Phoenix. The renter from the cold open. Same apartment. Same block.]

She signed a lease last month. Her rent went up again. Not by much. A nudge.

The algorithm — or its successor, under a new name, owned by the same company, trained on the same data pool — made that nudge.

And as of today, nobody has paid a number large enough to stop it.
