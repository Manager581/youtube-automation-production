Two days ago, a company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit. The DOJ proved they built an algorithm coordinating rent prices across ten million apartments — taking confidential landlord data and telling every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed. Rents went up nationwide.

The settlement? Zero dollars. A company making one point six billion a year walked away free. Algorithm still running. Your rent still high.

How? What system produces that? The answer is a formula corporate America has perfected since 1977: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. I'm tracing this formula from invention to the version running now — one we might never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

1977. A memo that should have killed a company. The Ford Pinto had a fuel tank that ruptured in crashes, catching fire. Ford knew. Imagine the boardroom: fix costs one hundred and thirty-seven million. Lawsuits from letting people burn? Forty-nine point five million. Ford chose forty-nine million. A real memo, in a real courtroom. They valued a life at two hundred thousand — using the government's own number.

It worked. People died. Settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial for reckless homicide ended in acquittal. In 1977, this was front-page scandal. You'd expect the system to shut it down. Instead, corporate America learned: the formula works. If you get away with killing people, what can't you get away with?

Forty-two years later, someone tested that.

In 2019, the FTC levied the largest privacy fine ever: five billion. Meta — then Facebook — had signed a consent decree in 2012, a parole deal with the government to stop deceiving users about their data. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica — a firm harvesting profiles for voter manipulation — paid for eighty-seven million users' data without their knowledge. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion. The FTC called it historic. Same day, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up. Nine percent of one year's revenue — a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket if you make a hundred thousand a year, for something that made you billions. The stock gained seven billion in market cap. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing.

Somewhere in Washington, a regulator wrote a press release about accountability. On Wall Street? Champagne. You can't write satire this good.

Ford proved the formula works but was punished reputationally. Meta raised the stakes: the market applauds you for getting caught. Punishment became a feature. Every company noticed.

But both were about money. Nobody died. Does the formula survive death?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer.

October 2018: Lion Air 610 crashes. One hundred eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines 302. One hundred fifty-seven dead. Same plane, same flaw — MCAS, a system forcing the nose down on a faulty sensor. Boeing knew. Engineers flagged it before certification, calling it a plane "designed by clowns, supervised by monkeys." Fix meant re-certification — regulators independently verifying the plane's safety. Cost: half a billion to a billion. So Boeing hid the flaw and shipped the plane. MCAS killed three hundred forty-six people.

Settlement: two and a half billion. Airlines got most of it, not families. No executive jailed. Boeing's revenue: fifty-eight billion a year. Three hundred forty-six lives cost sixteen days of income. Then Boeing violated the settlement. They broke the deal about the law they broke that killed those people. Still making planes. Stock recovered.

Death didn't break the formula.

After the memo, the planes, the five-billion-dollar fine that made Facebook richer — you know what changed? Press releases got longer. Congress held hearings where senators asked tough questions, then broke for lunch. The system built an elaborate performance of accountability that looks exactly like justice and does nothing. Theater with a trillion-dollar budget and a standing ovation from Wall Street.

I almost admire it. Almost.

But in each case, someone chose. One boardroom, one decision. Different question: what happens when nobody chooses? When the formula becomes culture — five thousand three hundred people committing fraud simultaneously, none thinking they're the problem?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million fake accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without consent. Five thousand three hundred employees, because the quotas were impossible to meet honestly. Management built a machine. Fraud was the output.

Five thousand three hundred fired. CEO retired with full benefits. Three billion in fines. Eighty billion in revenue. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud.

I want to slow down here. Because what comes next is different. Not stock prices, not planes, not fake accounts. A quiet, wealthy family who understood the formula better than anyone — and used it to kill more Americans than every other case combined.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. They knew OxyContin was highly addictive. Internal documents prove they knew people were dying. They pushed higher doses. More than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses — more than Baltimore's population.

In 2025, the Sacklers paid seven point four billion. Personal wealth: eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin on a product that killed six hundred thousand people. Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand. The Sacklers paid twelve thousand per death. The formula got cheaper.

Five cases. Fifty years. Every fine a fraction of the profit — zero percent to twenty-one, with the highest requiring six hundred thousand deaths and fifty states. One trillion in corporate fines since 2000. A hundred twenty-seven companies paying over a billion each, repeatedly. Repeat offenders get smaller fines. The system rewards recidivism.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

But every case shares one feature: violation first, fine after. What if an industry studied this playbook, did the math upfront, and decided to break the law before the first product shipped?

That industry is AI. The formula has reached its final form.

Ninety copyright lawsuits as of March 2026. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major company sued for scraping the internet without permission, training models on billions of copyrighted works they never paid for.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, recall the car. With Meta, delete the data. With Boeing, ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a newspaper from Claude. Every lawsuit could succeed and the models survive. The fine is just the purchase price for stolen data.

Anthropic: settled for one point five billion after training on millions of pirated books. Valuation: sixty billion. Settlement: two point five percent. Model still running. OpenAI: New York Times sued for billions. While courts crawl, they raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred fifty-seven billion. Lawsuits are background noise. Perplexity: Reddit set a trap — a hidden post. Perplexity surfaced it. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's citations went up forty-fold. Scraped more after being told to stop.

Scrape now. Train. Make it indispensable. Settle later. And the next generation is trained on you — your conversations, emails, photos, voice. The models will already be built when the lawsuits land.

"Move fast and break things" never meant software. It meant the law.

Does it have to be this way? No. Someone proved it doesn't.

The EU's GDPR: four percent of global annual revenue. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, Meta changed how they operate across Europe. The math changed. Behavior followed.

Proportional fines work. Criminal liability works — executives face prison. Disgorgement works — return every dollar. Structural remedies work — breakups, enforceable decrees, industry bans.

If the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. If executives face prison, the decision changes. It's just math.

But until the math changes, every company runs the formula Ford wrote in 1977 — Meta validated, Boeing tested with human lives, Wells Fargo industrialized, the Sacklers pushed to its extreme, and every AI company perfects right now.

They'll break the law. Make their money. Pay the fine.

Because breaking the law is profitable. Until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Pinto memo is forty-nine years old. The formula has been perfected, not eliminated. Will the fine be less than the profit?

The only thing that breaks it is math. Not outrage. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop — not because they find their conscience, but because they find a new spreadsheet.

We know what works. The question is why we haven't done it.

Now you know the math.
