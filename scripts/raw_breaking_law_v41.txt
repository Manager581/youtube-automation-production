Last month, Marquita Davis lost her apartment. Not because she stopped paying rent. Because an algorithm decided she should pay more.

Marquita is a medical assistant in Dallas. Two kids, seven and eleven. Same unit for three years, never missed a payment. Then her rent jumped thirty percent overnight. Twelve hundred to fifteen hundred dollars. Same apartment. Same job. She moved her kids into her mother's living room.

Before I show you the new version of this formula — the one that might be impossible to undo — I need to show you the old one. Fast. Because the pattern has been running for fifty years and the math has never changed.

In 1977, Ford discovered the Pinto's fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. People burned to death. Ford's engineers found the flaw before the car shipped. Someone in the boardroom wrote a memo. Fixing every Pinto on the road: a hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. Expected wrongful death payouts: forty-nine point five million. They shipped the car. This is where the formula was written. If the fine is less than the profit, proceed.

Meta proved it scales. In 2018, Facebook let a quiz app harvest the data of eighty-seven million users — names, locations, likes, messages, political leanings — and sell it to Cambridge Analytica, who turned it into voter-targeting weapons. Facebook had signed a consent decree promising to stop exactly this. They broke it anyway. The FTC fined them five billion dollars. Sounds massive. It was nine percent of one year's revenue. The day the fine was announced, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up. The market added seven billion in value. The punishment made Facebook two billion dollars richer than if the government had done nothing. Your data was in that harvest. Not hypothetically. Actually. If you had a Facebook account in 2018, your information was packaged and priced and the total cost to the company that sold it made them more valuable.

Here's a thought experiment. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally immune for a year? Speed everywhere. Skip your taxes. Park wherever you want. The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing. That's the deal Meta got. Except they didn't ask you first.

Boeing proved the formula survives death. October 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed thirteen minutes after takeoff. A hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302. Purdue Pharma proved you can move the money first. In Kermit, West Virginia — population four hundred — a single pharmacy received twelve million hydrocodone pills in three years. They moved the money. The settlement: seven point four billion. That works out to about twelve thousand dollars per death. Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand in 1977. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper.

Five decades. Five industries. Every fine was less than the profit. Every company survived. That was the old formula.

With AI, there is no off switch.

Scraped data is woven into a model during training like sugar dissolved in water. You cannot un-dissolve it. You cannot identify which outputs came from which training inputs. You cannot extract Sarah's writing style from the model any more than you can extract the eggs from a baked cake. The violation is the product. Every response the AI generates, every image it creates, every line of code it writes — all of it is built on data that was taken without permission and can never be returned.

Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit — all ninety of them — they keep the models.

The fine doesn't punish the theft. It becomes the retroactive purchase price for data that was already consumed. Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable to millions of users. Settle later. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the cost of raw materials, paid after the factory is already built and running.

Trillions of words consumed. And by the time the legal system catches up — years from now, maybe a decade — the models will be infrastructure. As embedded in daily life as electricity. Too big to shut down. Too valuable to punish meaningfully.

"Move fast and break things" was never about software. It was about the law.

So is the formula unbeatable?

No. Because someone already broke it.

Because four percent of global revenue made the violation more expensive than the profit for the first time.

Proportional fines. Criminal liability for executives, not the people at the bottom. Disgorgement — forcing companies to return every dollar earned from the violation.
