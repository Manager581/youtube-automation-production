Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge was extraordinary: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing with each other — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a single cent. No financial penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for renters who paid inflated prices for years.

In Dallas, a woman named Marquita Davis watched her rent jump thirty percent in a single year — from twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She couldn't afford it. She moved her two kids into her mother's living room. She wasn't alone. Across the country, renters were being squeezed by the same invisible hand, and none of them knew it.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? What kind of system produces that outcome?

Here's what I found when I started pulling the thread. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. A formula so simple it fits in one sentence: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's the entire operating system.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how each version made the next one inevitable, and why the newest version might be the one we can never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. And it starts with a memo that should have killed a company.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap, popular compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death. Ford's engineers identified the flaw before the car ever went to market.

Imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers say there's a fix. It costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to retrofit every Pinto on the road. But the lawsuits from people burning to death? Only forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This was a real document, presented in a real courtroom. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number pulled directly from the government's own guidelines. They used the government's math to justify letting their customers burn.

And it worked exactly as predicted. People died. Families sued. The settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was recalled. But the calculation had proven itself.

Now — in 1977, this was a genuine scandal. Front-page news. Public outrage. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. So Ford paid a different price: its reputation. For decades, every executive in America understood that even if the formula worked financially, you'd be dragged through the press and your brand would carry the stain.

That should have been enough. But it created a new question: what if you could use the formula and skip the reputational cost entirely? What if the market didn't punish you — what if it rewarded you?

Forty-two years later, Meta answered that question. And the answer changed everything.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012 — essentially a parole deal with the government, a binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica paid for access to eighty-seven million Facebook users' personal data. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To make that real: if you earn a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. But here's the real punchline — the stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing.

Think about that for a second. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally allowed to break any rule you wanted for a year? Would you speed everywhere? Skip your taxes? Park in handicapped spots? The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing — and that's exactly what Facebook concluded too. They just didn't ask permission.

This is where the formula evolved. Ford proved the math works, but Ford still got punished in public. Meta proved something far more dangerous: you can get caught, pay the fine, and come out richer. The market doesn't just tolerate the formula. It celebrates it. And once Wall Street started pricing fines as a cost of doing business rather than a sign of failure, every corporate boardroom in America got the same message: the only risk is not being big enough.

So the formula survived money. It survived privacy violations. It survived public outrage. But there was still one line nobody had crossed. One moral cost that should have been too high. Does the formula survive when people actually die — not in a seventies compact car, but in a modern commercial aircraft, in broad daylight, with the whole world watching?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that question.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four years old, on her way to Kenya to work with a health NGO. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who built consumer safety law in America. She died on Ethiopian 302. Her family sued. And in court, they proved what everyone suspected: Boeing knew.

Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." But fixing it meant re-certification — having federal regulators independently verify the plane was safe. Cost: five hundred million to one billion. Boeing had watched Ford survive the Pinto. They'd watched Meta's stock go up after a five-billion-dollar fine. So they hid the flaw. Shipped the plane. And MCAS killed three hundred and forty-six people.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion. Criminal penalties: two hundred and forty-three million. One point seven seven billion to airline customers — not victims' families, the airlines. Five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison.

Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. The cost of three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue. Then Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered. Death didn't break the formula. Not even close.

Now — Ford created the formula. Meta proved the market rewards it. Boeing proved that mass death doesn't stop it. Each one of these made the next one thinkable. But they all share one thing: someone in a boardroom made a choice. So here's the question that kept me up: what happens when the formula becomes automatic? When it's not a decision anymore — just the way things work?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Five thousand three hundred employees — because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. Management built the machine. Fraud was the output. When it came out, the workers were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Annual revenue: eighty billion. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud. Wells Fargo got caught, paid, got caught again, paid again, got caught a third time. Still the fourth-largest bank in America. The punishment isn't a deterrent. It's a subscription fee.

You know what's quietly remarkable about that? We've reached the point where a company can commit fraud for fourteen years, get caught three times, and the only lasting consequence is a federal asset cap that prevents it from getting bigger. Not smaller. Just not bigger. That's the ceiling of corporate punishment in America.

But none of these cases answers the darkest version of the question. I want to slow down here, because what comes next is different.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain — paying respected physicians like Dr. Russell Portenoy, once called the King of Pain, to tell their peers that fewer than one percent of patients would become addicted. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." By then, millions were hooked.

The Sacklers pushed higher doses and made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the family agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth — extracted before Purdue went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. Thirty-five billion in lifetime revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. About twelve thousand dollars per death. In 1977, Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper.

Ford. Meta. Boeing. Wells Fargo. The Sacklers. Five decades. Every fine was less than the profit. Every company survived. Every executive went home. Since 2000, corporations have paid over one trillion dollars in penalties. And repeat offenders receive smaller fines as a percentage of revenue than first-timers. The system rewards doing it again — the legal term is recidivism, and it's supposed to make punishment worse, not better.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Now — every case so far shares one thing: the violation happened first, the fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta harvested the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid. But what if an entire industry studied this fifty-year playbook, did the math in advance, and decided to break the law before the first product shipped? Not as a side effect. As the business model itself.

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

As of March 2026, ninety active copyright lawsuits target AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney — all sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, and selling models trained on stolen material for billions.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. Even if every company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

Sarah Silverman was one of the first to sue. She and two other authors proved that OpenAI had ingested their books without permission. In September 2025, Anthropic settled a separate class action for one point five billion after authors proved the company downloaded millions of pirated books to train their AI. Largest AI copyright recovery in history. But Anthropic's valuation was over sixty billion. Two point five percent. The model trained on those pirated books? Still running.

Meanwhile, OpenAI raised another six point six billion in funding while being sued by the New York Times for billions. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits aren't slowing anything down. They're background noise. And the next generation of models is being trained on something more personal than books — your conversations with chatbots, your emails scanned by AI assistants, your voice cloned by tools you signed up for without reading the terms. When those lawsuits come, the models will already be trained. The settlement will be a fraction of the profits. Just like every time before.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law.

So — does it have to be this way? No. The EU proved it doesn't. GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue — not a subsidiary, everything worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta restructured operations across all of Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because four percent annually made privacy violations unprofitable for the first time. Proportional fines, criminal liability for the executives who sign off, disgorgement — that means you don't just pay a penalty, you return every dollar earned from the violation. When the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. The math has to change. And the EU showed it can.

Until then, every company runs the same formula Ford wrote in 1977. They break the law. They make their money. They pay the fine. Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

"If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class." That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
