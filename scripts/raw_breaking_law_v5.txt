On March 27th, 2026 — two days ago — a company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The DOJ proved that RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars. No penalty. No admission of guilt. A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue walked away without paying a single cent.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? Not a reduced fine. Not a slap on the wrist. Nothing. What kind of system produces that outcome?

The answer is a calculation — one that corporate America has been perfecting for nearly fifty years. Will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. I'm going to trace this formula from the moment it was invented to the version running right now, one that might be impossible to reverse.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

It starts in 1977. Not in a courtroom. In a memo.

The Ford Pinto was a compact car with a fuel tank that ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death inside their vehicles. Ford's engineers knew about the flaw before the car went to market. But instead of fixing it, someone did something that would change corporate behavior forever — they put a price tag on human life.

The memo: one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to fix every Pinto on the road. Forty-nine point five million in expected lawsuits if they didn't.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This wasn't rumor. This document was presented in court. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number they pulled directly from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's own guidelines. They used Washington's math to justify letting their customers burn.

So what happened? Exactly what the memo predicted. People died. Families sued. But Ford had already done the arithmetic — the settlements cost less than the fix would have. The criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was eventually recalled, but the calculation had already proven itself. The memo was right. Breaking the law was cheaper.

Corporate America's takeaway? Not "don't let people die." The takeaway was: run the numbers first. And that should have been the end of it — a scandal so grotesque that regulators would close the loophole forever.

Instead, it became standard practice. Because the formula didn't just survive. It evolved.

Forty-two years later, in July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission — the agency responsible for protecting American consumers — announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Meta, then called Facebook, had signed what's called a consent decree in 2012. That's a legally binding agreement with the government — in this case, a formal promise to stop deceiving users about how their data was shared. Facebook broke that promise. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica — a company that helped political campaigns micro-target voters — had paid for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million Facebook users without their knowledge. Facebook knew this was happening and allowed it, because selling access to user data was enormously profitable.

The fine: five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic. Record-breaking.

On the same day, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To put that in terms you can feel: if you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For something that made you billions.

The five-billion-dollar fine wasn't a deterrent. It was a price tag — the exact cost of violating your users' privacy. And the price was reasonable.

But here's where this crosses from infuriating into genuinely absurd. The stock gained seven billion in market cap that same day. The punishment made the company worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing at all. Somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability. On the trading floor, champagne. You can't write satire this good — reality already did.

So Ford invented the formula. Meta proved the market will reward you for using it. But those are cases where the only casualties are money and privacy. What happens when three hundred and forty-six people are in the air?

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw. A system called MCAS — short for Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

Boeing knew. Their engineers flagged the problem before the plane was ever certified. Internal messages called it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." But fixing the software properly meant going back to the FAA for re-certification — the process where regulators verify that a plane is safe to fly. Estimated cost: five hundred million to one billion dollars. So instead of re-certifying, Boeing hid the flaw. They shipped the plane anyway. And because the fix was never made, the same failure killed one hundred and eighty-nine people, and then five months later killed one hundred and fifty-seven more.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion. But not for the families — one point seven seven billion went to airline customers, two hundred and forty-three million in criminal penalties, five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison. Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion — a hundred and fifty-nine million per day. The cost of three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue.

And then Boeing violated the terms of that settlement. They broke the agreement about the law they broke that killed those people. Still one of two companies that build commercial aircraft. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

Ford invented the formula. Meta proved it pays. Boeing proved it holds even when people die. But notice — these are all individual companies, making individual choices. Therefore the question becomes: what if the formula stops being a choice one company makes and starts being the culture an entire institution runs on?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Think about that number. Not three point five million dollars. Three point five million fake accounts — credit cards nobody applied for, checking accounts opened without permission, fake email addresses enrolling people in services they never requested. And not by a handful of rogue employees. Five thousand three hundred people did this. The quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. So five thousand three hundred people cheated — because that was the only way to keep their jobs.

This is different from Ford or Boeing. Those were top-down decisions by executives. Wells Fargo turned the formula into a machine. Management set impossible targets, workers committed fraud to meet them, and when it all came out, five thousand three hundred workers were fired for doing exactly what the culture demanded. The CEO? Retired. Full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Eighty billion in annual revenue. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of income — for fourteen years of fraud against three point five million people.

By now, you might be feeling something familiar. A kind of heaviness. Another case, another set of numbers, another CEO who walked away fine. If you're starting to feel numb — that's the system working. Numbness is the product. When outrage never converts into structural change, it's just free public relations. The company rides out the news cycle, and the formula holds.

So I need you to stay with me for this next one. Because this is where the formula crosses a line.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents show they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They knew people were dying. They funded campaigns to change how doctors thought about pain. They pushed higher doses. And they made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. That's more than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the Sacklers agreed to pay seven point four billion in a settlement with every state in the union. Their estimated personal wealth — pulled from Purdue before it went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was criminally charged. They shielded themselves through corporate structure and emerged with billions intact.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin. On a product that killed six hundred thousand people.

If that's justice, what does injustice look like?

Now zoom out. Because individually, each case looks like a bad actor. Together, they reveal a machine.

RealPage: one point six billion in revenue. Penalty: zero.
Meta: fifty-five point eight billion. Penalty: five billion. Nine percent.
Boeing: fifty-eight point two billion. Penalty: two point five billion. Four percent.
Wells Fargo: eighty billion. Penalty: three billion. Four percent.
Purdue: thirty-five billion lifetime. Settlement: seven point four billion. Twenty-one percent — the highest, and it took six hundred thousand deaths and fifty states to get there.

Since 2000, one trillion dollars in corporate fines. One hundred and twenty-seven companies paying more than a billion each — repeatedly. And repeat offenders get smaller fines as a percentage of revenue, not larger. The system rewards recidivism.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Every case I've shown you has one thing in common: the violation happened first, and the fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta collected the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid. But what if an entire industry looked at this fifty-year track record, did the math in advance, and decided to break the law as a deliberate business strategy?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the calculation has never been more naked.

As of March 2026, ninety active copyright lawsuits against AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major player sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, and using all of it to train models they sell for billions.

Here's what makes AI the final evolution of this formula. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. The scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract the New York Times from Claude. Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

Anthropic settled a class action in September 2025 for one point five billion — the largest AI copyright recovery in history — after authors proved they'd trained on millions of pirated books. Anthropic's valuation: over sixty billion. The settlement: two point five percent. The model? Still running.

In January 2026, Universal Music, Concord, and ABKCO filed another suit — three point one billion. Lawsuits stack up. Model keeps running. Money keeps flowing.

OpenAI: the New York Times sued for billions. A federal judge ordered twenty million anonymized ChatGPT logs turned over — largest discovery order in AI history. While the legal process crawls forward, OpenAI raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

And then there's Perplexity. Reddit set a trap — a post visible to Google but otherwise hidden. Hours later, Perplexity surfaced material from it. Caught. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's response? Citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop. Because the data is worth more than any fine.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later for a fraction. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the cost of raw material taken without asking.

And the next generation of models — training right now — are being trained on you. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails scanned by AI assistants. Your photos. Your voice. Data more personal than anything before. When the lawsuits come, the models will already be built, the products already shipping, the settlement a fraction of the profits.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship the product. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

So — does it have to be this way?

No. And we know this because someone already fixed it.

The European Union's GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not the subsidiary. Not last quarter. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error — it was a restructuring event. Meta's entire business model changed in Europe. The math changed. The behavior followed.

Proportional fines work. Criminal liability for executives works — the people who signed off face prison, not just a corporate check. Disgorgement works — you return every dollar earned from the violation. Structural remedies work — forced breakups, enforceable consent decrees, industry bans for repeat offenders.

None of this is radical. It's just math. If the penalty exceeds the profit, the calculation changes. If executives face prison, the decision changes. If the fine is four percent of global revenue every year until compliance, the incentive changes.

But until the math changes, every company will keep doing exactly what Ford did in 1977, what Meta did in 2019, what Boeing did when those planes were in the air, what the Sacklers did while six hundred thousand people died, and what every AI company is doing right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Ford Pinto memo was written forty-nine years ago. The practice it invented hasn't been eliminated. It's been perfected. But the question at its heart — will the fine be less than the profit? — hasn't changed once.

The only thing that changes it is math. Not outrage. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop. Not because they become moral. Because they become rational in a different direction.

We know what works. The question is why we haven't done it.

Now you know the math.
