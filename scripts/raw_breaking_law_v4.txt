On March 27th, 2026 — two days ago — a company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The DOJ proved that RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars. No penalty. No admission of guilt. A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue walked away without paying a single cent.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? Not a reduced fine. Not a slap on the wrist. Nothing.

The answer is a calculation — one that corporate America has been perfecting for nearly fifty years. Will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. I'm going to trace this formula from the moment it was invented to the version running right now, one that might be impossible to reverse.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

It starts in 1977. Not in a courtroom. In a memo.

The Ford Pinto was a compact car with a fuel tank that ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death inside their vehicles. Ford's engineers knew about the flaw before the car went to market. And then someone did something that would change corporate behavior forever — they put a price tag on human life.

The memo: one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to fix every Pinto on the road. Forty-nine point five million in expected lawsuits, burn injuries, and vehicle damage if they didn't.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This wasn't rumor. This document was presented in court. Ford's internal analysis valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — using the government's own number from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. They used Washington's math to justify letting their customers burn.

Lawsuits came. People died. The Pinto was recalled. A criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. Ford paid settlements that cost less than the fix would have.

Corporate America's takeaway? Not "don't let people die." The takeaway was: run the numbers first.

Now — you'd think this would have been a turning point. That regulators would have closed the loophole. That putting a dollar value on human life would have become corporate suicide.

Instead, it became standard practice. And forty-two years later, the math got even better.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Meta — then Facebook — had signed a consent decree in 2012, a legally binding promise to stop deceiving users about their privacy. They broke it. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica had paid for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million users without their knowledge, and Facebook let it happen because sharing that data was enormously profitable.

The fine: five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic. Record-breaking.

On the same day, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up.

Investors didn't see a punishment. They saw certainty. The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To put that in human terms: if you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions.

The five-billion-dollar fine wasn't a deterrent. It was a price tag. It told every company in America exactly what privacy violations cost. And the price was reasonable.

But here's the detail that makes this almost comedy. The stock gained seven billion in market cap that same day. Seven billion. The punishment made the company worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing at all. Wall Street literally threw a party. Somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability, and on the trading floor, people were popping champagne. You can't write satire this good. Reality already wrote it.

That's what happens when the only thing at risk is money and reputation. But what happens when three hundred and forty-six people are in the air and the risk is their lives?

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same software flaw. A system called MCAS — Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — that pushed the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

Boeing's engineers flagged the problem before the plane was certified. Internal messages called it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." Fixing the software properly meant re-certification — five hundred million to one billion dollars. So Boeing hid the flaw from the FAA. They shipped the plane. And three hundred and forty-six people died.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion. Two hundred and forty-three million in criminal penalties, one point seven seven billion to airline customers — not the families, the airlines — and five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison. Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. A hundred and fifty-nine million per day. Three hundred and forty-six deaths cost them sixteen days of revenue.

And then the DOJ found that Boeing violated the terms of that settlement. They broke the agreement about how they broke the law that killed those people. Still one of two companies that build commercial aircraft. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

So the formula survives even when people die. But all of these — Ford, Meta, Boeing — are individual companies making individual decisions. What happens when the formula doesn't just infect a company, but becomes the operating system of an entire institution?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created approximately three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Fake email addresses used to enroll people in services they never requested. Not a few rogue employees. Five thousand three hundred of them — because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. The pressure was so intense that cheating was the only way to survive.

Five thousand three hundred employees were fired for doing what the culture demanded. The CEO? Retired. Full benefits.

Total fines: three billion dollars. Annual revenue: eighty billion. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud against three point five million people.

Now I want to pause here. Not for emphasis — for honesty. Because there's something that happens when you line up these cases one after another. You start to go numb. The numbers get bigger. The violations get worse. And at a certain point your brain just — stops. Another billion. Another scandal. Another CEO who retired comfortably. It starts to feel like weather. Like something that just happens.

That numbness? That's the system working. That's the whole point. If outrage never converts into structural change, then outrage is just free public relations. The company weathers the news cycle, the stock dips and recovers, and the formula holds.

So I need you to not be numb for this next one. Because this is where the calculation crosses a line that doesn't have a name.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents show they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They knew people were dying. They funded campaigns to change how doctors thought about pain. They pushed higher doses. And they made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the Sacklers agreed to pay seven point four billion in a settlement with every state in the union. Their estimated personal wealth — pulled from Purdue before it went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was criminally charged. They shielded themselves through corporate structure and emerged with billions intact.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin. On a product that killed six hundred thousand people.

If that's justice, what does injustice look like?

Now zoom out. Because individually, each case looks like a bad actor. Together, they reveal a machine.

RealPage: one point six billion in revenue. Penalty: zero.
Meta: fifty-five point eight billion. Penalty: five billion. Nine percent.
Boeing: fifty-eight point two billion. Penalty: two point five billion. Four percent.
Wells Fargo: eighty billion. Penalty: three billion. Four percent.
Purdue: thirty-five billion lifetime. Settlement: seven point four billion. Twenty-one percent — the highest, and it took six hundred thousand deaths and fifty states to get there.

Since 2000, one trillion dollars in corporate fines. One hundred and twenty-seven companies paying more than a billion each — repeatedly. And repeat offenders get smaller fines as a percentage of revenue, not larger. The system rewards recidivism.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

But everything I've shown you has one thing in common: the violation happened first, and the fine came after. What if an entire industry looked at this track record — Ford, Meta, Boeing, Wells Fargo, Purdue — did the math in advance, and decided to break the law as a business strategy? Not by accident. By design.

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the calculation has never been more explicit.

As of March 2026, ninety active copyright lawsuits against AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major player sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, and using all of it to train models they sell for billions.

Here's what makes AI different from every other case on this list. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. The scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract the New York Times from Claude. Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

Anthropic settled a class action in September 2025 for one point five billion — the largest AI copyright recovery in history — after authors proved they'd trained on millions of pirated books. Anthropic's valuation: over sixty billion. Settlement: two point five percent. The model trained on those books? Still running.

In January 2026, another lawsuit — Universal Music, Concord, and ABKCO — for three point one billion. Lawsuits stack up. Model keeps running. Money keeps flowing in.

OpenAI: sued by the New York Times for billions. A federal judge ordered twenty million anonymized ChatGPT logs handed over — largest discovery order in AI history. While that grinds through the courts, OpenAI raised another six point six billion. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

Perplexity: Reddit set a trap — a post visible to Google but otherwise hidden. Hours later, Perplexity surfaced it. Caught. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's response? Their citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later for a fraction. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the cost of raw material taken without asking.

And the next generation of models — training right now — are being trained on you. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails. Your photos. Your voice. Data more personal than anything before. When the lawsuits come, the models will already be built, the products already shipping, and the settlement a fraction of the profits.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship the product. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

So. Does it have to be this way?

No. And we know this because someone already fixed it.

The European Union's GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not the subsidiary. Not last quarter. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error — it was a restructuring event. Meta's entire business model changed in Europe. The math changed. The behavior followed.

That's the proof of concept. Proportional fines work. Criminal liability for executives works — not just the company paying, but the people who made the decision facing prison. Disgorgement of profits works — returning every dollar earned from the violation, not just paying a fraction. Structural remedies work — forced breakups, consent decrees with real enforcement, industry bans for repeat offenders.

None of this is radical. It's just math. If the penalty exceeds the profit, the calculation changes. If executives face prison, the decision changes. If the fine is four percent of global revenue every year until compliance, the incentive changes.

But until the math changes, every company will keep doing exactly what Ford did in 1977, what Meta did in 2019, what Boeing did when those planes were in the air, what the Sacklers did while six hundred thousand people died, and what every AI company is doing right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Ford Pinto memo was written forty-nine years ago. The practice it invented hasn't been eliminated. It's been perfected. But the fundamental question — will the fine be less than the profit? — hasn't changed once.

The only thing that changes it is math. Not outrage. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop. Not because they become moral. Because they become rational in a different direction.

We know what works. The question is why we haven't done it.

Now you know the math.
