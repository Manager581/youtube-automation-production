Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge was extraordinary: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing with each other — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company generating one point six billion dollars in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a single cent. No financial penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for the renters who paid inflated prices for years.

In Dallas, a woman named Marquita Davis watched her rent jump thirty percent in a single year — from twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She couldn't afford it. She moved her two kids into her mother's living room. She wasn't the only one. Across the country, renters were being squeezed by the same invisible hand, and none of them knew it.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? Not a reduced fine. Not a slap on the wrist. Nothing. What kind of system produces that outcome?

Here's what I found when I started pulling the thread. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. A formula so simple it fits in one sentence: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's the entire operating system.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how it was tested, how it survived even when hundreds of people died — and why the newest version, happening right now, might be the one we can never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. And it starts with a memo that should have killed a company.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap, popular compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank was positioned so that it ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death inside their vehicles. Ford's engineers identified the flaw before the car ever went to market.

Now imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers have just told you there's a fix. It costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to retrofit every Pinto on the road. But the lawsuits from people burning to death? The wrongful death settlements, the burn injury payouts, the vehicle replacement costs? Those will only total forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This wasn't rumor or speculation. This was a real document, presented in a real courtroom. Ford's internal cost-benefit analysis explicitly valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number they pulled directly from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's own guidelines. They used the government's math to justify letting their customers burn.

And it worked exactly as the memo predicted. People died. Families sued. But the settlements cost less than the fix would have. A criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times in American history that a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was eventually recalled. But the calculation had already proven itself.

In 1977, this was a genuine scandal. Front-page news. A corporation putting a dollar value on human life was shocking. So you'd expect the system to have closed this loophole by now. But it didn't. Because the lesson corporate America learned was not "don't kill people." The lesson was: the formula works. Run the numbers first. And that raised a dangerous question — if you can get away with it when people die, what can't you get away with?

Forty-two years later, someone tested that question. And the answer was breathtaking.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. The target was Meta, then called Facebook. In 2012, after getting caught deceiving users about their privacy, Facebook signed a consent decree — a legally binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica — a firm that harvested personal profiles to build voter manipulation tools — paid for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million Facebook users without their knowledge. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic. Record-breaking. A message to every company in America.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To put that in terms you can feel: if you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. But the real punchline is bigger. The stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing at all.

Now think about that. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally allowed to break any rule you wanted for a year? Would you speed everywhere? Skip your taxes? Park in handicapped spots? Of course you would. The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing — and that's exactly what Facebook concluded too. Except they didn't ask permission. They just did the math.

Ford proved the formula works, but Ford was at least punished reputationally. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. Meta raised the stakes: what if you use the formula and the market applauds you for getting caught? Meta didn't just survive punishment. It profited from it. And every company in America noticed.

Which raises the next question — one that should genuinely scare you. The formula survived money. It survived privacy violations. It survived public outrage. But does it survive death? Is there a moral cost high enough to break the math?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that question.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. All one hundred and eighty-nine people on board were killed. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor reading.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four years old, on her way to Kenya to work with a health NGO. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who built consumer safety law in America. She died on Ethiopian 302. Her family sued. And in court, their lawyers proved what everyone suspected: Boeing knew.

Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." But fixing it properly meant re-certification — the process where federal regulators independently verify that a plane is safe to fly. Cost estimate: five hundred million to one billion dollars. So instead of going back to the FAA, Boeing hid the flaw. They shipped the plane. And MCAS killed three hundred and forty-six people.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion dollars. Two hundred and forty-three million in criminal penalties. One point seven seven billion to airline customers — not the victims' families, the airlines. Five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison.

Boeing's annual revenue, even in a pandemic year: fifty-eight point two billion. The cost of three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue. Then Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered. The formula held. Death didn't break it.

And after all this — the Pinto memo, two planes falling out of the sky, the five-billion-dollar fine that made Facebook richer — the system changed nothing. The press releases got longer. Congress held hearings where senators asked tough questions and then broke for lunch. The regulatory apparatus built this elaborate performance of accountability that looks exactly like justice and does absolutely nothing.

But the formula was still, in each case, a decision. One company. One boardroom. Someone chose. So here's a different question: what happens when nobody chooses? When the formula becomes so embedded in an institution that five thousand three hundred people commit fraud simultaneously?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created approximately three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Not a handful of rogue employees. Five thousand three hundred of them — because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly.

When it all came out, five thousand three hundred workers were fired for doing what the culture demanded. The CEO? Retired. With his full benefits.

Three billion dollars in fines. Annual revenue: roughly eighty billion. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of systematic fraud.

You know what's quietly amazing about this story? Wells Fargo got caught, paid the fine, got caught again, paid another fine, got caught a third time — and is currently under a federal asset cap that prevents it from growing. A repeat offender, punished repeatedly. And it's still the fourth-largest bank in America. Still profitable. Still open. The punishment isn't a deterrent. It's a subscription fee.

But none of these cases answers the darkest version of the question. And I want to slow down here, because what comes next is different. It's about a family who understood the formula better than anyone — and used it to kill more Americans than every other case I've mentioned combined.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain — including paying key opinion leaders, respected physicians who were given grants and speaking fees to tell their peers that opioid addiction fears were overblown. Dr. Russell Portenoy, once called the King of Pain, spent years telling other doctors that fewer than one percent of patients would become addicted. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." By then, millions were already hooked.

The Sacklers pushed doctors to prescribe higher doses. And they made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore. Gone.

In 2025, the Sackler family agreed to pay seven point four billion dollars. Their estimated personal wealth — money they extracted before Purdue went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. They shielded themselves through corporate structure and emerged with billions intact.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. The Sacklers paid about twelve thousand per death. In 1977, Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper.

Now zoom out. Ford. Meta. Boeing. Wells Fargo. The Sacklers. Five decades. Five industries. Millions of victims. Every fine was less than the profit. Every company survived. Every executive went home.

Since 2000, American corporations have paid over one trillion dollars in penalties. One hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid more than a billion. Repeatedly. And the repeat offenders — the ones caught again and again — receive smaller fines as a percentage of revenue than first-timers. The system literally rewards recidivism — that's the legal term for getting caught doing the same thing over and over.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Every case I've shown you has one thing in common. The violation happened first. The fine came after. But what if an entire industry studied this fifty-year playbook, did the math in advance, and decided to break the law as a deliberate business strategy — before the first product even shipped?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

As of March 2026, there are ninety active copyright lawsuits against AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company is being sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works — books, articles, images, music, code — and using all of it to train models they sell for billions.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

In September 2025, Anthropic settled a class action for one point five billion dollars. Authors proved Anthropic had downloaded millions of pirated books to train their AI. The largest AI copyright recovery in history. But Anthropic's valuation was over sixty billion. The settlement was two point five percent of the company's value. The model trained on those pirated books? Still running.

OpenAI's situation is even more brazen. The New York Times sued for billions. In January 2026, a federal judge ordered OpenAI to hand over twenty million anonymized ChatGPT logs — the largest discovery order in AI history. While the legal process grinds on, OpenAI raised another six point six billion in funding. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

And Perplexity — Reddit set a trap. They created a post visible only to Google's crawler but otherwise hidden. Within hours, Perplexity's AI surfaced material from that post. Caught. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's response? Their citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They didn't stop scraping. They scraped more. Because the data is worth more than any fine.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later for a fraction. The model can't be untrained.

And the next generation is being trained on you. Not books or articles. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails scanned by AI assistants. Your photos processed by AI editors. Your voice cloned by tools you signed up for without reading the terms. The data being collected right now is more personal and more valuable than anything before. And when the lawsuits come, the models will already be trained. The settlement will be a fraction of the profits. Just like every time before.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship the product. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

So — does it have to be this way? No. The European Union proved it doesn't. GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue — not a subsidiary, not one quarter, everything worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error. Meta changed how they operate across all of Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because the math changed. Proportional fines. Criminal liability for executives. Disgorgement — meaning you don't just pay a fine, you return every dollar earned from the violation. When the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks.

But until the math changes, every company will keep running the same formula Ford wrote in 1977. They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine. Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

There's a comment that went viral under a video about this subject. One sentence: "If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class." That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
