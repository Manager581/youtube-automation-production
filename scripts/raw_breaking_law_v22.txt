Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge was extraordinary: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing with each other — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a single cent. No financial penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for renters who paid inflated prices for years.

In Dallas, a woman named Marquita Davis watched her rent jump thirty percent in a single year — from twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She couldn't afford it. She moved her two kids into her mother's living room. She wasn't alone. Across the country, renters were being squeezed by the same invisible hand, and none of them knew it.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? What kind of system produces that outcome?

Here's what I found. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. A formula so simple it fits in one sentence: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's the entire operating system.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how each version made the next one inevitable, and why the newest version might be the one we can never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. And it starts with a memo that should have killed a company.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap, popular compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. The car caught fire. People burned to death. Ford's engineers found the flaw before the car went to market. And then someone in the boardroom did the math.

The fix costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. The lawsuits from people burning to death? Only forty-nine point five million. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number pulled directly from the government's own guidelines. They used the government's math to justify letting their customers burn. The memo recommended against the fix.

And it worked as predicted. People died. Families sued. The settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was recalled. But the calculation had proven itself.

In 1977, this was a genuine scandal. Front-page news. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. And here's the thing — it should have been the end. Because even though the formula worked financially, it destroyed Ford's reputation. For decades, every executive in America understood there was a cost the spreadsheet couldn't capture. You might survive the fine. But you'd never survive the front page.

That created the next question: what if you could use the formula and the front page didn't matter?

Because that's exactly what happened. Forty-two years later.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012 — that's essentially a parole deal with the government, a binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica paid for access to eighty-seven million Facebook users' personal data. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To make that real: if you earn a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. But here's the punchline — the stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing.

Think about that. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally allowed to break any rule you wanted for a year? Would you speed everywhere? Skip your taxes? Park in handicapped spots? The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing — and that's exactly what Facebook concluded too. They just didn't ask permission.

Somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability. On the trading floor? Champagne. You genuinely cannot write satire this good. Reality already wrote it.

And that's where the formula evolved into something truly dangerous. Because Ford got punished in the press. The Pinto was a cautionary tale. But Meta showed every boardroom in America that the cautionary tale was obsolete. You can get caught, pay the fine, and come out richer. Wall Street doesn't price fines as failure. It prices them as overhead.

Now I want to step back for a second. Because up to this point, we're talking about money and data. Enormous amounts of money and data. But nobody died. The formula has been tested on profit and privacy, and it passed both times. So the question hanging over everything is the obvious one: does the formula survive when people actually die? Is there a body count high enough to break the math?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS. Here's what it did: a single sensor on the outside of the plane measured the angle of the nose. If it thought the nose was too high, MCAS automatically forced it down. The problem was the sensor could fail — and when it failed, the system shoved the nose toward the ground and the pilots couldn't override it.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four years old, on her way to Kenya to work with a health NGO. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who built consumer safety law in America. She died on Ethiopian 302. Her family sued. And in court, they proved what everyone suspected: Boeing knew.

Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." Fixing it meant re-certification — having federal regulators independently verify the plane was safe. Cost: five hundred million to one billion. So Boeing hid the flaw. Shipped the plane. And MCAS killed three hundred and forty-six people.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion. A victim fund of five hundred million — split among three hundred and forty-six families. No executive went to prison. Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. Sixteen days of revenue. Then Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

Death didn't break the formula.

Now every case so far was still a choice — one boardroom, one memo, one decision. So here's where it gets genuinely unsettling: what happens when the formula stops being a choice and becomes the culture? When thousands of people commit fraud simultaneously, and nobody thinks they're the villain?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Five thousand three hundred employees — because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. Management built the machine. Fraud was the output. When it came out, the workers were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Annual revenue: eighty billion. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud. The government put Wells Fargo under an asset cap — a limit that prevents the bank from growing larger. Not smaller. Just not larger. That's the ceiling of corporate punishment in America. Wells Fargo is still the fourth-largest bank in the country.

I want to slow down here. Because everything I've shown you so far is, in some sense, impersonal. Boardroom math. Stock tickers. Settlement numbers. But what comes next is different. It's about a family who understood the formula better than anyone on this list — and used it to kill more Americans than every other case I've mentioned combined.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain — paying respected physicians like Dr. Russell Portenoy, once called the King of Pain, to tell their peers that fewer than one percent of patients would become addicted. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." By then, millions were hooked.

The Sacklers pushed higher doses and made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the family agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth — extracted before Purdue went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. Thirty-five billion in lifetime revenue. About twelve thousand dollars per death. In 1977, Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Now — every case so far shares one thing: the violation happened first, the fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta harvested the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid. But what if an entire industry studied this fifty-year playbook, did the math in advance, and decided to break the law before the first product shipped — not as a side effect, but as the business model itself?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

Here's the core insight: with Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Every major AI company scraped the internet without permission, ingested billions of copyrighted works, and trained models they now sell for billions. As of March 2026, there are ninety active lawsuits. But scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. Even if every company loses every case, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

In September 2025, Anthropic settled for one point five billion after authors proved the company downloaded millions of pirated books. Largest AI copyright recovery in history. Anthropic's valuation: over sixty billion. The settlement was two point five percent. The model? Still running. Meanwhile, OpenAI raised six point six billion more while being sued by the New York Times. The lawsuits are background noise.

And the next generation of models is being trained on something more personal — your conversations with chatbots, your emails scanned by AI assistants, your voice cloned by tools you signed up for without reading the terms. When those lawsuits come, the models will already be trained. The settlement will be a fraction of the profits.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law.

So — does it have to be this way? No. The European Union proved it doesn't. They passed a law called GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — and it does one thing differently: fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta restructured operations across all of Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because four percent annually made privacy violations unprofitable for the first time. The behavior changed overnight. Not because of morality. Because of math.

Proportional fines. Criminal liability for the executives who sign off. Disgorgement — meaning you don't just pay a penalty, you return every dollar earned from the violation. When the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks.

But until the math changes, every company runs the same formula. And Marquita Davis, the woman in Dallas who lost her apartment to an algorithm — she's still at her mother's house. The algorithm is still running. And nobody paid anything.

"If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class." That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
