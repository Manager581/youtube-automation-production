Two days ago, a company you've probably never heard of settled one of the most consequential lawsuits in American history. And the outcome should terrify you — not because of what they did, but because of what it tells us about the system.

The company is called RealPage. The DOJ proved they built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords and told every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep.

The settlement was zero dollars. Not reduced. Not negotiated down. Zero.

How is that possible? What kind of system lets a company fix rent for ten million apartments and charges nothing?

The answer is a formula — one that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. Will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's it. I'm going to trace this formula from the moment it was invented to the version running right now — one that might be permanently irreversible.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. It starts with a single memo.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap compact car — had a design flaw. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death. Ford's engineers knew before the car went to market.

Imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers say there's a fix — one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to retrofit every Pinto on the road. But the lawsuits from letting people burn? Those will only cost forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. Ford chose the forty-nine million.

This was a real memo, presented in court. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — using the government's own number. Washington's math, used to justify letting customers burn.

What happened next proved the formula right. People died. Families sued. But the settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial — one of the only times a corporation has faced reckless homicide charges — ended in acquittal. The math worked exactly as the memo predicted.

In 1977, this was a scandal. Front-page news. A corporation putting a price tag on human life was genuinely shocking. So you'd expect the system to have closed this loophole by now. But it didn't close it. It studied it. Because the lesson corporate America learned was not "don't kill people." The lesson was: the formula works. Now who else can use it?

That question got its answer forty-two years later — and the answer was worse than anyone imagined.

In 2019, the Federal Trade Commission — the government agency responsible for protecting consumers — levied the largest privacy fine in history. Five billion dollars. The target: Meta, then called Facebook. Here's what Facebook had done. In 2012, after getting caught deceiving users about their privacy, Facebook signed what's called a consent decree — a legally binding agreement with the government, like a parole deal, where you promise to change your behavior or face serious consequences. Facebook signed it, then broke it. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica had been paying for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million users without their knowledge, and Facebook allowed it because the money was too good. They violated their own parole.

Five billion. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up. The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. If you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket for something that made you billions. The stock gained seven billion in market cap that same session — two billion more than the fine. The punishment made the company richer than doing nothing would have.

The five-billion-dollar fine wasn't a deterrent. It was a price tag. And here's where it crosses from infuriating into genuinely absurd: somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability. On the trading floor? Champagne. The government tried to punish Facebook, and Wall Street sent them a thank-you card.

So Ford proved the formula works — but Ford was punished, at least reputationally. Therefore Meta raised the stakes: what if you use the formula and the market actually rewards you? What if getting caught breaking the law makes your stock go up? Meta proved that the formula doesn't just survive punishment. It thrives on it.

But — and this is critical — both Ford and Meta involved money. Privacy. Stock prices. Nobody died. So the obvious next question is: does the formula break when human lives are on the line? Is there a point where the math can't hold because the moral cost is too high?

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — one hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw. A system called MCAS — Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

Boeing knew. Their engineers flagged the problem before the plane was certified. Internal messages called it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." But fixing it properly meant re-certification — the process where federal regulators independently verify a plane is safe to fly. Cost: five hundred million to one billion dollars. So instead of going back to the FAA, Boeing hid the flaw and shipped the plane. Because the flaw was never fixed, MCAS killed one hundred and eighty-nine people — and then five months later killed one hundred and fifty-seven more.

The settlement: two and a half billion. Mostly paid to airlines, not families. No executive went to prison. Boeing's revenue: fifty-eight point two billion a year — a hundred and fifty-nine million per day. Three hundred and forty-six lives: sixteen days of revenue.

Then Boeing violated the settlement itself. Broke the deal about the law they broke that killed those people. Still one of two companies making commercial aircraft. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

The formula held. Even with three hundred and forty-six people dead, the math worked.

Ford created the formula. Therefore Meta tested whether the market would punish it — and the market applauded. Therefore Boeing tested whether death would break it — and it didn't. Therefore the next question becomes: what happens when the formula isn't a single decision by a single CEO, but a culture? What happens when five thousand three hundred people all use it simultaneously?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million fake accounts. Not three point five million dollars — three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. And not a handful of rogues — five thousand three hundred employees, all doing the same thing, because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. The pressure was so intense that cheating was the only way to keep your job. Management built a machine, and fraud was the output.

Five thousand three hundred workers were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Eighty billion in revenue. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of income — for fourteen years of fraud against three point five million people.

I want to pause here. Not for dramatic effect — for honesty. Because I know what's happening to you right now. You're going numb. Four companies, each one worse. The numbers get bigger, the consequences get smaller, and at some point your brain just stops processing. Another billion. Another CEO who walked away fine. It starts to feel like weather.

That numbness is the system working as designed. Outrage that never converts into change is just free PR.

So I need you to stay with me for one more case. Because this is where the formula crosses a line that doesn't have a name yet.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They knew people were dying. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain. They pushed higher doses. And they made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the Sacklers agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth — extracted from Purdue before it went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was criminally charged.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin. On a product that killed six hundred thousand people.

If that's justice — what does injustice look like?

Now zoom out. Because alone, each case looks like a bad actor. Together, they're a machine.

RealPage: one point six billion in revenue. Penalty: zero.
Meta: fifty-five point eight billion. Five billion. Nine percent.
Boeing: fifty-eight point two billion. Two point five billion. Four percent.
Wells Fargo: eighty billion. Three billion. Four percent.
Purdue: thirty-five billion lifetime. Seven point four billion. Twenty-one percent — the highest, and it took six hundred thousand deaths and fifty states to get there.

Since 2000, one trillion dollars in corporate fines. A hundred and twenty-seven companies each paying over a billion — repeatedly. Repeat offenders get smaller fines, not larger. The system rewards recidivism.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

But every case so far shares one feature: the violation happened first. The fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta harvested the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid.

What happens when an industry studies this fifty-year track record and decides to break the law before the products even launch — not because they got caught up in the moment, but because the formula told them to?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

As of March 2026, ninety active copyright lawsuits. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company, sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, training models on content they never paid for.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a newspaper from Claude. Even if every lawsuit succeeds, the models survive. The fine becomes the purchase price for stolen data.

Anthropic settled for one point five billion in September 2025 — largest AI copyright recovery ever — after authors proved they trained on millions of pirated books. Valuation: over sixty billion. Settlement: two point five percent. The model? Still running.

January 2026: Universal Music sued for three point one billion. Lawsuits stack. Model keeps running.

OpenAI: the New York Times sued for billions. A federal judge ordered twenty million ChatGPT logs turned over. While the courts crawl, OpenAI raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

Perplexity: Reddit set a trap — a hidden post only visible to Google. Hours later, Perplexity surfaced it. Caught. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the cost of raw material taken without asking.

And the next generation — training now — is trained on you. Your chatbot conversations. Your emails. Your photos. Your voice. When the lawsuits come, the models will already be built.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship it. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

So — does it have to be this way?

No. Someone already proved it doesn't.

The EU's GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Not one quarter. Everything. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error — it was a restructuring event. Meta changed their entire European operation. Not because they wanted to. Because the math changed.

Proportional fines work. Criminal liability for executives works — the person who signs off faces prison, not just the company writing a check. Disgorgement works — returning every dollar earned from the violation. Structural remedies work — breakups, enforceable decrees, industry bans for repeat offenders.

If the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. If executives face prison, the decision changes. If the fine is four percent of global revenue every year until compliance, the incentive flips. None of this is radical. It's just math.

But until the math changes, every company will keep running the same formula — the one Ford wrote in 1977, Meta validated, Boeing tested with human lives, Wells Fargo industrialized, the Sacklers pushed to its extreme, and every AI company is perfecting right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Pinto memo is forty-nine years old. The formula it created has been perfected, not eliminated. But the question at its heart hasn't changed: will the fine be less than the profit?

The only thing that breaks it is math. Not outrage. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop — not because they find their conscience, but because they find a new spreadsheet.

We know what works. The question is why we haven't done it.

Now you know the math.
