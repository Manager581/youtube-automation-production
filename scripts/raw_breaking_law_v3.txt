On March 27th, 2026 — two days ago — a company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The DOJ proved that RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars. No penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for renters. A company generating one point six billion dollars in annual revenue walked away without paying a single cent.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But here's what nobody is asking. How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? Not a reduced fine. Not a slap on the wrist. Nothing. What kind of system produces that outcome?

The answer is a calculation. One that corporate America has been perfecting for nearly fifty years. And once you see it, you can't unsee it: Will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. I'm going to trace this calculation from the moment it was invented to the version running right now — one that might be impossible to reverse.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

It starts in 1977. Not in a courtroom. In a memo.

The Ford Pinto was a compact car with a fuel tank that ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death inside their vehicles. Ford's engineers identified the flaw before the car ever went to market. And then someone in a corner office did something that would change corporate behavior forever. They put a price tag on it.

The memo calculated the cost of fixing every Pinto on the road: one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. Then it calculated the expected cost of wrongful death lawsuits, burn injuries, and vehicle damage: forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This wasn't a leaked rumor. This document was presented in court. Ford's internal analysis explicitly valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a figure they got from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's own guidelines. They used the government's own number to justify letting their customers burn.

Lawsuits came. People died. The Pinto was eventually recalled. The criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times in American history a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. Ford paid civil settlements that were, collectively, a fraction of what the fix would have cost.

The lesson corporate America took from the Ford Pinto was not "don't let people die." The lesson was: run the numbers first.

But that was 1977. Surely the system learned something in the next forty-two years?

It learned that the numbers work even better than Ford imagined.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission — the government agency responsible for protecting consumers — announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Meta, then called Facebook, had been caught violating a consent decree. A consent decree is a legally binding promise to the government — in this case, a promise Facebook had already made to stop deceiving users about their privacy. They broke that promise when a political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica paid Facebook for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million users without their knowledge. Facebook realized that sharing user data with third parties was enormously profitable, and the legal agreement they'd signed was just paper.

The fine: five billion dollars.

Five billion. A number designed to make you feel like justice was served. The FTC called it historic. Record-breaking. A message to every company in America.

But on the same day the fine was announced, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up. Investors didn't see a punishment — they saw certainty. The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. If you make a hundred thousand dollars a year and get a nine-thousand-dollar fine for breaking the law, would that stop you? Especially if what you did made you billions?

The five-billion-dollar fine wasn't a deterrent. It was a pricing signal. It told every company in America exactly how much it costs to violate your users' privacy. And that cost turned out to be extremely reasonable.

Now — here's where this stops being just infuriating and starts being almost funny. Almost. The day your government announces the largest corporate privacy punishment in American history, Wall Street throws a party. The stock gained seven billion in market cap in a single trading session — two billion more than the fine itself. It's like getting a speeding ticket and finding a twenty in the envelope. The punishment made the company richer. If the FTC had done nothing at all, Facebook's shareholders would have been worse off.

I don't know whether to laugh or scream. So let's do neither. Let's just follow the math.

Meta proved the market rewards lawbreaking. But that's what happens when the only thing at risk is money. What happens when three hundred and forty-six people are in the air?

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. All one hundred and eighty-nine people on board were killed. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same software flaw. A system called MCAS — short for Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — that pushed the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

This is a calculation story, not an accident story. Boeing's engineers flagged the MCAS problem before the plane was certified. Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." They knew. But fixing the software would have required re-certification — estimated cost: five hundred million to one billion dollars. So Boeing hid the flaw from the FAA. They shipped the plane. And three hundred and forty-six people died.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion dollars. Two hundred and forty-three million in criminal penalties, one point seven seven billion to airline customers — not the victims' families, the airlines — and five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison.

Boeing's annual revenue, even in a pandemic year: fifty-eight point two billion. A hundred and fifty-nine million per day. The settlement for three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue.

And then the DOJ found that Boeing violated the terms of that settlement. They broke the agreement about how they broke the law that killed three hundred and forty-six people. Boeing is still one of only two companies that build commercial aircraft. Still getting government contracts. The stock recovered.

Ford proved the calculation works. Meta proved the market rewards it. Boeing proved that even when hundreds of people die, the math holds. But all three are single companies making single decisions. So what happens when the calculation infects an entire institution — not one bad executive, but five thousand three hundred employees all doing the same thing?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created approximately three point five million unauthorized accounts. They signed customers up for credit cards they never applied for. Opened checking accounts without permission. Generated fake email addresses to enroll people in online banking they never requested. Five thousand three hundred employees did this — not because they were criminals, but because Wells Fargo's corporate culture demanded it. They had quotas. The pressure to hit those quotas was so intense that the only way to survive was to cheat.

Five thousand three hundred people were fired for doing what management incentivized them to do. The CEO retired with his full benefits.

Total fines: approximately three billion dollars. Annual revenue: roughly eighty billion. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of revenue for fourteen years of systemic fraud against three point five million customers.

Ford, Meta, Boeing, Wells Fargo. Each one escalated the calculation. But notice what's still true: people survived. Renters still had homes. Users still had Facebook. Passengers — most of them — still flew safely. Customers eventually got their money back.

So what happens when six hundred thousand people don't survive?

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents show they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They knew people were dying. They funded campaigns to change how doctors thought about pain. They pushed higher doses. And they made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the Sacklers agreed to pay seven point four billion dollars in a settlement with every state in the union. But their estimated personal wealth — money pulled out of Purdue before it went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was criminally charged. They filed for bankruptcy through the company, shielded themselves through corporate structure, and emerged with billions intact.

The math: thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. That's a seventy-nine percent profit margin on a product that killed six hundred thousand people.

If that's justice, what does injustice look like?

Now zoom out. Because individually, each case looks like a bad company doing a bad thing. Together, they're a system.

RealPage: one point six billion in revenue. Penalty: zero. Zero percent.
Meta: fifty-five point eight billion. Penalty: five billion. Nine percent.
Boeing: fifty-eight point two billion. Penalty: two point five billion. Four point three percent.
Wells Fargo: eighty billion. Penalty: three billion. Three point eight percent.
Purdue: thirty-five billion lifetime. Settlement: seven point four billion. Twenty-one percent — the highest on this list, and it took six hundred thousand deaths and fifty states to get there.

Since 2000, American corporations have paid over one trillion dollars in fines. One hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid more than a billion. Not once — repeatedly. And repeat offenders receive smaller fines as a percentage of their revenue than first-time offenders. The system literally rewards recidivism.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Everything I've shown you so far has one thing in common: the violation happened, the damage was done, and the fine was assessed after the fact. But what if I told you there's an industry right now — today, in 2026 — that looked at every case on this list, did the math in advance, and decided to break the law anyway? Not because they got caught up in the moment. Because they planned it.

I'm talking about artificial intelligence.

As of March 2026, there are ninety active copyright lawsuits against AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company is being sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works — books, articles, images, music, code — and using all of it to train models they sell for billions of dollars.

But here's what makes AI different from every other case. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI, the violation is the product. The scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract the New York Times from Claude. The training data is inseparable from the thing being sold. Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

In September 2025, Anthropic settled a class action for one point five billion dollars after authors proved they'd downloaded millions of pirated books from shadow libraries to train their AI. The largest AI copyright recovery in history. But Anthropic's valuation: over sixty billion. The settlement was two point five percent of the company's value. The model? Still running.

In January 2026, Universal Music, Concord, and ABKCO filed another suit — three point one billion. The lawsuits stack up. The model keeps running. Anthropic keeps raising money.

OpenAI's situation is even more brazen. The New York Times sued for billions. A federal judge ordered OpenAI to hand over twenty million anonymized ChatGPT logs — the largest discovery order in AI history. While the legal process grinds on, OpenAI raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

And then there's Perplexity. Reddit set a trap — a post indexable by Google but otherwise invisible. Within hours, Perplexity surfaced material from it. Caught. But after Reddit sent a cease-and-desist in May 2024, Perplexity's citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop. Because the data is worth more than any fine.

Scrape now. Ingest the world's knowledge. Train the model. Make it indispensable. When the lawsuits come, settle for a fraction. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the price of raw material you took without asking.

It's the Ford playbook perfected. Because AI companies aren't pretending anymore. They've watched forty-nine years of proof that the math works.

And the next generation of models — being trained right now — are being trained on you. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails scanned by AI assistants. Your photos. Your voice. Data more personal and more valuable than anything before. When the lawsuits come, the models will already be trained. The settlement will be a fraction of the profits. Just like every time before.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship the product. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

But here's the question this entire video has been building toward. Does it have to be this way?

No.

The European Union already proved it doesn't. GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not the subsidiary. Not last quarter. Everything. When Ireland's Data Protection Commission levied a one point two billion euro fine against Meta in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error — it was a restructuring event. Meta's entire business model had to change in Europe. The math changed. The behavior followed.

Proportional fines. Criminal liability for executives — not just the company paying, but the people who signed off facing prison. Disgorgement of profits — you don't just pay a fine, you return every dollar earned from the violation. Structural remedies — forced breakups, consent decrees with teeth, industry bans for repeat offenders.

None of this is radical. It's just math. If the penalty exceeds the profit, the calculation changes. If executives face prison, the decision changes. If the fine is four percent of global revenue every year until compliance, the incentive changes.

But until the math changes, every company will keep doing exactly what Ford did in 1977, what Meta did in 2019, what Boeing did when three hundred and forty-six people were in the air, what the Sacklers did while six hundred thousand people died, and what every AI company is doing right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Ford Pinto memo was written in 1977. Forty-nine years ago. The practice it invented hasn't been eliminated. It's been perfected. The spreadsheets got smarter. The lawyers got more expensive. The settlements got more precisely calibrated. But the fundamental question — will the fine be less than the profit? — hasn't changed once.

The only thing that changes it is math. Not outrage. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop. Not because they become moral. Because they become rational in a different direction.

We know what works. The question is why haven't we done it.

Now you know the math.
