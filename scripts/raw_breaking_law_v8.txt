Two days ago, a company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the DOJ. And the outcome should terrify you.

RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across ten million apartments. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords and told every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep.

The settlement? Zero dollars. A company making one point six billion a year walked away without paying a cent.

How? What kind of system lets a company fix rent for ten million apartments and charges nothing?

The answer is a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. I'm going to trace this formula from its invention to the version running right now — one that might be permanently irreversible.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

It starts in 1977. With a memo.

The Ford Pinto had a fuel tank that ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. Ford's engineers knew before it went to market. Imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers say the fix costs one hundred and thirty-seven million. But the lawsuits from letting people burn? Only forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. Ford chose the forty-nine million.

This was a real memo, presented in court. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — using the government's own number. Washington's math, used to justify letting customers burn.

What happened? People died. Families sued. But the settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial — one of the only times a corporation faced reckless homicide charges — ended in acquittal. The formula worked exactly as written.

In 1977, this was a scandal. A corporation pricing human life was front-page shocking. You'd expect the system to close this loophole. Instead, it studied the formula. Because the lesson wasn't "don't kill people." The lesson was: the math works.

And forty-two years later, someone figured out how to make it even better.

In 2019, the Federal Trade Commission — the agency protecting consumers — levied the largest privacy fine in history. Five billion dollars against Meta, then called Facebook. Here's what happened: Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012 — essentially a parole agreement with the government, a legally binding promise to stop deceiving users about their privacy data. They broke it. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica — a company that harvested personal data to build voter manipulation tools — paid for access to eighty-seven million users' profiles without their knowledge, and Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up. The stock gained seven billion in market cap — two billion more than the fine. The punishment made Facebook richer than if the government had done nothing at all.

If you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, this was a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket for something that made you billions. Somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability. On the trading floor? Champagne. You can't write satire this good.

Ford proved the formula works — but Ford was at least punished reputationally. People were outraged. Meta showed that you don't even need to hide it anymore. The market will applaud you for getting caught, as long as the fine is predictable. Punishment became a feature, not a bug.

But both Ford and Meta involved money — privacy, stock prices, data. Nobody died. So here's the question that should keep you up at night: does the formula break when human lives are on the line?

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — one hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw. A system called MCAS that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

Boeing knew. Engineers flagged it before certification. Internal messages called it a plane "designed by clowns, supervised by monkeys." But fixing it meant re-certification — the process where regulators independently verify a plane is safe. Cost: five hundred million to one billion. So Boeing hid the flaw and shipped the plane. MCAS killed one hundred and eighty-nine people. Then, because the fix still wasn't made, it killed one hundred and fifty-seven more.

Settlement: two and a half billion. Mostly paid to airlines, not families. No executive imprisoned. Boeing's revenue: fifty-eight billion a year. Three hundred and forty-six lives cost them sixteen days of revenue.

Then Boeing violated the settlement itself. They broke the deal about the law they broke that killed those people. Still making planes. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

There's a moment in every one of these stories that I keep thinking about. Not the big number. Not the settlement. It's the moment before. The meeting where someone knew. Where an engineer wrote "supervised by monkeys" and hit send, knowing nothing would change. Where a pharmacist looked at the prescription numbers and felt something was wrong. The formula works because at every step, someone's job depends on not asking the next question.

Ford created the formula. Meta proved the market rewards it. Boeing proved it survives even when people die. But in each case, the formula was a choice in a single boardroom.

What changes when it's not a decision anymore — when it's a culture? When five thousand three hundred people all use the formula at once?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million fake accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without consent. Not a handful of rogues — five thousand three hundred employees, because the quotas were impossible to meet honestly. Management built the machine. Fraud was the output.

Five thousand three hundred workers fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Eighty billion in revenue. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud.

Now — I know what's happening. You're going numb. Four cases, each worse. The numbers get bigger, the consequences shrink. Another billion. Another CEO walking away fine. It feels like weather.

That numbness is the point. When outrage never becomes structural change, it's just free PR.

So stay with me. Because this next one is where the formula stops being about negligence or culture and becomes something that doesn't have a name.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. They knew OxyContin was highly addictive. Internal documents prove they knew people were dying. They pushed higher doses. They made billions while six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the Sacklers agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth — extracted from Purdue before bankruptcy — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin on a product that killed six hundred thousand people.

I keep coming back to the number two hundred thousand. That's what Ford said a human life was worth in 1977. Forty-nine years later, the Sacklers paid about twelve thousand per death. We went backwards. The formula didn't just survive. It got cheaper.

Zoom out. Ford. Meta. Boeing. Wells Fargo. Purdue. Five cases spanning fifty years. In every single one, the fine was a fraction of the profit. Zero percent for RealPage. Nine percent for Meta. Four percent for Boeing. Four percent for Wells Fargo. Twenty-one percent for Purdue — and that took six hundred thousand deaths and every state in the union.

Since 2000, American corporations have paid one trillion dollars in fines. A hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid over a billion — repeatedly. And here's the detail that completes the picture: repeat offenders get smaller fines, not larger. The more you break the law, the less it costs. The system rewards recidivism.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

But every case so far shares one feature: the violation came first, the fine after. What happens when an industry studies this track record, does the math in advance, and decides to break the law as a deliberate strategy — before the products even launch?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

Ninety active copyright lawsuits as of March 2026. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company, sued for scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, training models on content they never paid for.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a newspaper from Claude. Even if every lawsuit succeeds, the models survive. The fine is just the purchase price for stolen data.

Anthropic settled for one point five billion in September 2025 after authors proved they trained on millions of pirated books. Valuation: sixty billion. Settlement: two point five percent. The model? Still running.

January 2026: Universal Music sued for three point one billion. Lawsuits stack. Model runs.

OpenAI: the New York Times sued for billions. A judge ordered twenty million ChatGPT logs turned over. While the courts crawl, OpenAI raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. Lawsuits are background noise.

Perplexity: Reddit set a trap — a hidden post only Google could find. Perplexity surfaced it within hours. Caught. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later. The fine is just the cost of raw material taken without asking. And the next generation — training now — is trained on you. Your conversations. Your emails. Your photos. Your voice.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship it. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

Does it have to be this way? No. Someone already proved it doesn't.

The EU's GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, Meta changed how they operate across Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because the math changed.

Proportional fines work. Criminal liability works — executives face prison, not just corporate checks. Disgorgement works — you return every dollar. Structural remedies work — breakups, enforceable decrees, industry bans.

If the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. If executives face prison, the decision changes. None of this is radical. It's just math.

But until the math changes, every company will keep running the formula Ford wrote in 1977 — the one Meta validated, Boeing tested with human lives, Wells Fargo industrialized, the Sacklers pushed to its extreme, and every AI company is perfecting right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Pinto memo is forty-nine years old. The formula has been perfected, not eliminated. But the question at its heart hasn't changed: will the fine be less than the profit?

The only thing that breaks it is math. Not outrage. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop — not because they find their conscience, but because they find a new spreadsheet.

We know what works. The question is why we haven't done it.

Now you know the math.
