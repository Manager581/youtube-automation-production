Something is following you. Right now, as you watch this, a number you have never seen is quietly deciding what your life looks like. Not your credit score — you know about that one. This is something else. Something hidden. And the companies that created it? You've never heard of them. But they have heard of you. They know where you've lived, who you've lived with, what you've bought, what you owe, and what you did on a Tuesday in 2014. They have assembled a portrait of you so detailed it would make a stalker blush — and they have reduced that portrait to a single number. A secret score. One that is, right now, shaping where you can live, where you can work, whether your insurance claim gets paid, and whether a doctor will treat you. And until this moment, nobody told you it existed.

But here is the question nobody is asking. What happens when a perfect record means nothing?

Mary Louis paid her rent on time for sixteen years. Sixteen years. Not a single missed payment, not a single late check, not once. She was, by every reasonable measure, the kind of tenant any landlord should dream of. So when she applied for a new apartment, she expected what anyone would expect — approval.

She got a rejection letter.

No explanation. No human being she could call. Just a form that said, essentially, no. And the thing that denied her wasn't a person. It wasn't a landlord who looked at her application and made a judgment call. It was a number — generated by a company she had never heard of, using data she had never been asked to verify. A ghost in the machine said Mary Louis wasn't good enough. Sixteen years of proof said otherwise. The ghost won.

So who built the ghost?

The company is called SafeRent, formerly known as CoreLogic. Their product is a tenant screening score — a single number that tells a landlord whether you're worth the risk. Sounds reasonable on the surface. Landlords need to make decisions. But here's what they don't tell you. SafeRent's algorithm doesn't just look at your rental history. It pulls from an enormous web of data — court records, eviction filings, debt records — and crunches all of it into a score. A score that, according to a federal lawsuit, systematically discriminated against Black and Hispanic renters.

Let that land for a moment.

SafeRent settled that lawsuit for two point two seven five million dollars. They didn't admit wrongdoing. They never do. But the Department of Justice found that their algorithm produced scores that disproportionately harmed people of color — effectively encoding racial discrimination into a math equation and calling it objective.

And this is where the secret becomes truly dangerous. Mathematics is not neutral when the data it feeds on is poisoned. And the data is poisoned. It has been poisoned for decades, by a society that has never treated everyone equally. When you train an algorithm on historical data from an unequal world, you don't get fairness. You get inequality at scale. You get discrimination that moves at the speed of a computer, that processes thousands of applications per hour, that never gets tired, never feels guilty, and never has to look anyone in the eye.

But here's the part that should make your stomach drop.

Only three percent of renters know which company screened them. Three percent. That means ninety-seven out of every hundred people who get rejected by one of these algorithms don't even know who to be angry at. They don't know what data was used. They don't know what the score was. They don't know how to challenge it. They just know they didn't get the apartment. And they move on, carrying an invisible mark, applying to the next place, getting rejected again, never understanding why. It is Kafka's "The Trial" made real — except it's not fiction, and it's not happening to one person. It's happening to millions.

Now. Tenant screening is just one corner of this world. So what happens when you apply for a job?

Because the same logic — the same faith in algorithms, the same removal of human judgment, the same black-box scoring — has consumed the hiring process at hundreds of major companies. You've probably experienced it. You submit your resume online. You wait. You hear nothing. You assume a human read your application and decided you weren't qualified.

But what if nobody read anything?

Increasingly, that's the truth. Your resume was parsed by software, scored by an algorithm, and discarded before a person ever saw your name. Companies like HireVue took this even further — they asked candidates to record video interviews, not with a person, but with a camera. Then artificial intelligence analyzed your facial expressions, your word choice, your tone of voice, and generated a score. Your employability, reduced to the way your mouth moved when you answered a question about your greatest weakness.

Pymetrics did something similar with neuroscience-based games. Short cognitive tests that claimed to measure your potential through pattern recognition and reaction time. The future of hiring, they called it.

So what does that future actually look like? Ask Derek Mobley.

Derek is a Black man who applied to over a hundred and fifty jobs. He didn't just apply casually — he was systematic, persistent, desperate to find work. And he was rejected from every single one. Automatically. Without a human ever reviewing his application. At one point, records show he submitted an application at one fifty in the morning. That's not someone who isn't trying. That's someone who can't sleep because the system keeps saying no.

Derek filed a lawsuit alleging that the AI screening tools discriminated against him because of his race.

And here's what nobody expected. A study by the Brookings Institution found that when identical resumes were submitted to AI hiring tools, white-associated names were preferred eighty-five point one percent of the time.

Eighty-five percent.

That's not a bug in the system. That is the system. If you have a name that sounds white, the machine smiles at you more than eight times out of ten. If you don't, you're fighting uphill before a single human has read a word you've written. And the companies selling these tools will tell you the algorithm is race-blind, that it doesn't see color. But it doesn't need to see color. It sees zip codes. It sees the names of schools. It sees gaps in employment that correlate with incarceration, which correlates with race, which correlates with poverty, which correlates with everything else in a society built on inequality. The algorithm doesn't need to be racist. It just needs to be accurate — accurate to a world that already is.

Now I need to tell you about Kyle Behm. Because his story is the one that haunts me.

Kyle was a young man who struggled with bipolar disorder. He was in treatment. He was managing his condition. He was trying to do what everyone tells you to do — get back out there, get a job, move forward with your life. So he applied for entry-level positions. Hourly work. The bottom rung of the ladder, the place where everyone is supposed to get a start.

But every application included a personality test. A long, probing questionnaire about his moods, his feelings, his mental state — questions that, for someone with a mental health condition, were essentially a trap. Kyle failed every single one. Not because he couldn't do the work. Not because he wouldn't show up on time. But because an algorithm decided his personality profile — shaped by a condition he was actively treating — made him a risk.

The rejections piled up. The sense of worthlessness grew.

And Kyle Behm attempted suicide.

His father, a lawyer, investigated. He discovered the personality tests were manufactured by a company called Kronos, used by major retailers — companies you shop at every week — to screen out applicants at the front door. Not based on skills. Not based on experience. Based on answers that functioned, in practice, as an illegal mental health screening under the Americans with Disabilities Act. Kyle's father filed complaints with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission.

But here's the thing about fighting these systems. The companies have armies of lawyers. They have studies they funded themselves that say the tests are valid. They have decades of selling these tools to HR departments that don't understand the science but love the efficiency. One family against that machine — David and Goliath, except Goliath has a legal team and David is recovering from a suicide attempt.

So why does this keep happening? Because the data beneath all of it is deeper than you can imagine.

LexisNexis — you might recognize the name from legal databases — operates one of the largest consumer data operations in the world. They maintain eighty-three billion records on two hundred and eighty-two million people. Let me say that again. Eighty-three billion records. On a population of roughly three hundred and thirty million. That's over two hundred and fifty data points for every man, woman, and child in America. They know where you've lived, who you've lived with, what cars you've driven, what court cases you've been involved in, what properties you've owned, what debts you've carried. They compile all of this into products sold to landlords, employers, insurance companies, and law enforcement.

And you have almost certainly never been told what they have on you.

But what happens when the data is wrong?

This is where it gets truly dangerous — and it is wrong, far more often than anyone admits. Court records are full of errors. People with similar names get their files merged. Charges that were dismissed still show up as convictions. Debts that were paid off still appear as outstanding. The algorithms consuming this data can't know the difference. They just see the numbers.

Carmen Arroyo found this out the hard way. Carmen's son was in an institution — a fact that appeared in screening databases tied to her name. When she applied for housing, a charge that had been fully dismissed — not reduced, not pled down, dismissed — still appeared in her background check as an active concern.

Her housing application was denied. Not because of anything she had done. Not because of any threat she posed. But because a database carried a ghost of an accusation that no longer existed in the eyes of the law but very much existed in the eyes of the algorithm. Carmen Arroyo was being punished for something that, legally, never happened.

You might be thinking — surely someone is watching these companies. Surely there's oversight.

And the answer is — barely.

New York City passed Local Law 144, requiring companies to audit their AI hiring tools for bias. Sounds good — until you learn that audits are often conducted by firms the companies hire themselves, results are sometimes meaninglessly vague, and enforcement has no teeth. Illinois passed BIPA, giving people the right to sue over unconsented biometric data collection. Colorado passed an AI Act requiring disclosures and impact assessments. But these are three states and one city in a country of fifty states and thousands of jurisdictions. The vast majority of Americans have no legal protection against algorithmic discrimination. None.

And the money behind these systems? Staggering.

RealPage — a company providing software to landlords nationwide — was acquired for nine point six billion dollars. That's how much the market values the ability to set rents, screen tenants, and manage properties using algorithms. The Department of Justice later filed an antitrust lawsuit against RealPage, alleging its software enabled competing landlords to coordinate pricing — algorithmic collusion. Landlords feeding data into the same system, the system telling all of them to raise rents. Not because of supply and demand. Because an algorithm said so, and everyone trusted the algorithm.

Now consider what happened at Cigna, one of the largest health insurers in the country. What they found was something nobody expected. An investigation revealed Cigna used an automated system that denied over sixty thousand claims in a single month. Sixty thousand. One system. One month. These weren't complex cases requiring careful medical judgment. They were processed in bulk, at speed, by a machine optimized not for patient care but for the company's bottom line. And most of those sixty thousand people, faced with the exhaustion of appeals, simply gave up. The algorithm was counting on that.

This is the fundamental asymmetry at the heart of the secret score economy. The companies have unlimited resources, unlimited data, unlimited patience. You have a rejection letter you don't understand and a phone number that goes to voicemail. They operate at millions of decisions per day. You are one person trying to figure out why you were denied an apartment, a job, a medical procedure. When SafeRent wrongly rejects a qualified tenant, SafeRent loses nothing. The tenant loses the apartment. When an AI hiring tool wrongly screens out a qualified candidate, the company moves to the next applicant. The candidate sits up at one fifty in the morning, submitting another application, wondering what's wrong with them.

And that's perhaps the cruelest part of all. These systems don't just deny you opportunities. They make you blame yourself.

When you don't know there's a score — when you don't know there's an algorithm — you internalize the rejection. You think you interviewed poorly. You think your application was weak. You think something is wrong with you. And there isn't. There's something wrong with the system. But the system will never tell you that, because to the system, you don't exist. You are a row in a spreadsheet, a probability in a model, a risk score that either clears the threshold or doesn't. The machine doesn't care about sixteen years of perfect rent. The machine doesn't care about a hundred and fifty applications. The machine doesn't care that you're recovering from a suicide attempt and just need someone to give you a chance. The machine has no concept of a chance. It has thresholds.

So what can be done?

The honest answer is we are at the very beginning of figuring that out. The laws being passed are first attempts, early drafts of a regulatory framework that doesn't yet exist at the scale it needs to. Europe is further ahead with its AI Act. But even there, enforcement is an open question. The technology moves faster than the law, and the companies have every incentive to move fast and ask forgiveness later.

But here's what they don't want you to know. There are things you can do right now.

You can request your consumer files from LexisNexis, SafeRent, and CoreLogic. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, they are legally required to show you what they have. Most people never ask. Ask. You can dispute errors — and you should expect to find them, because at this scale, errors are not the exception but the norm. You can support legislation requiring algorithmic transparency — the right to know when an AI has made a decision about you and the right to challenge that decision with a human who can override it. You can demand that companies making billions off your data be held accountable when that data is wrong, when their algorithms discriminate, when their systems cause harm.

Because here's the thing about secret scores. They only work in the dark. They rely on your ignorance. They rely on you not knowing who SafeRent is, not knowing what LexisNexis has, not knowing that the application you spent two hours on was rejected by a machine before a human ever saw it.

The moment you know, the dynamic changes. Not completely. Not overnight. But fundamentally. Because a system that cannot survive scrutiny is a system that does not deserve your trust.

Mary Louis was a good tenant for sixteen years. Derek Mobley applied to a hundred and fifty jobs. Kyle Behm just wanted a chance to work. Carmen Arroyo was haunted by a charge that no longer existed. None of them knew about the scores. None of them knew about the algorithms. None of them knew who to fight or how to fight them. They were invisible to the systems that judged them, and the systems were invisible to them.

You are being scored right now. By companies you have never heard of, using data you never gave them, feeding algorithms you will never see. Those scores are deciding where you can live, where you can work, what medical care you receive, and how much you pay for the roof over your head. They are doing this at scale, at speed, and in the dark.

And the single most dangerous thing about all of it — the thing that makes the whole machine work — is that until this moment, you didn't know.

Now you do.

The scores are real. The harm is real. And that long, profitable silence — the silence they built their billions on — is the thing that needs to end first.
