On March 27th, 2026 — two days ago — a company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The DOJ proved that RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars. No penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for renters. A company generating one point six billion dollars in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that bought it for ten point two billion, walked away without paying a single cent.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But here's the thing — RealPage didn't invent this playbook. They inherited it. There's a calculation that corporate America has been perfecting for nearly fifty years, and once you see it, you'll recognize it everywhere: Will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's it. That's the entire operating system. And I'm going to trace it from the moment it was invented to the version running right now — one that might be impossible to reverse.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

It starts in 1977, with a memo that would be taught in every business ethics class in every MBA program in the world.

The Ford Pinto was a compact car with a fuel tank positioned in a way that made it vulnerable to rupture in rear-end collisions. When ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death inside their vehicles. Ford's engineers identified the flaw before the car ever went to market. And Ford did something unprecedented: they wrote it down. The memo calculated the cost of fixing every Pinto on the road — one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. Then it calculated the expected cost of wrongful death lawsuits, burn injuries, and vehicle damage — forty-nine point five million dollars.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This is not conspiracy theory. This is a document presented in court. Ford's internal analysis explicitly valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a figure they got from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's own guidelines. They used the government's number to justify letting their customers burn.

And here's what happened. Lawsuits came. People died. The Pinto was eventually recalled. The criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times in American history a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. Ford paid civil settlements that were, collectively, a fraction of what the fix would have cost.

The lesson corporate America took from the Ford Pinto was not "don't let people die." The lesson was: run the numbers first.

But here's what I want you to notice. In 1977, this was a scandal. It was shocking. A company putting a dollar value on human life was front-page news. Therefore, you'd expect the system to have closed this loophole by now.

It didn't. It made it normal.

Fast forward to 2019. Meta — then Facebook — had already been caught deceiving users about their privacy. They'd already promised the government they'd stop. They'd already signed a legally binding consent decree to protect user data. They broke that agreement. Not subtly. Not accidentally. They broke it because Cambridge Analytica came along and Facebook realized that sharing user data with third parties was enormously profitable.

The FTC announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. They called it historic. Record-breaking. A message to every company in America.

But here's what happened next. On the same day the fine was announced, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up. Investors didn't see a punishment — they saw certainty. The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. If you make a hundred thousand dollars a year and get a nine-thousand-dollar fine for breaking the law, would that stop you? Especially if what you did made you billions?

The market understood something the FTC didn't. Facebook could afford to break the law every single year and pay this fine every single year and still be one of the most profitable companies on earth. The five-billion-dollar fine wasn't a deterrent. It was a pricing signal. It told every company in America exactly how much it costs to violate your users' privacy.

And that cost, as it turns out, is extremely reasonable.

Now, I want you to sit with that for a second. Because there's something almost absurd about it. The day your government announces the largest corporate punishment in the history of privacy law, Wall Street throws a party. Investors literally celebrated. The stock gained seven billion in market cap in a single session — two billion more than the fine itself. It's like getting a speeding ticket and finding a twenty in the envelope. The system had become so predictable that punishment was priced in before it even arrived.

But that's what happens when the only thing at risk is money. Meta proved the market rewards lawbreaking. What happens when three hundred and forty-six people die?

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. All one hundred and eighty-nine people on board were killed. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same software flaw. A system called MCAS that pushed the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

But this is a calculation story, not an accident story. Boeing's engineers flagged the MCAS problem before the plane was certified. Internal messages show employees writing that the airplane was "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." They knew. But fixing the software properly would have required re-certification — estimated cost: five hundred million to one billion dollars. So Boeing hid the flaw from the FAA. They shipped the plane. One hundred and eighty-nine people died. And then — because the fix still hadn't been made — one hundred and fifty-seven more.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion dollars. But look at where the money went — two hundred and forty-three million in criminal penalties, one point seven seven billion to airline customers — not the victims' families, the airlines — and five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison.

Boeing's annual revenue, even in a pandemic year: fifty-eight point two billion dollars. That's a hundred and fifty-nine million per day. The settlement for three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue. The fix that would have saved those lives? Less than the settlement they ended up paying. They chose the more expensive option because it let them ship faster.

And then the DOJ found that Boeing violated the terms of that settlement. They broke the agreement about how they broke the law that killed three hundred and forty-six people. Boeing is still one of only two companies in the world that build commercial aircraft. Still getting government contracts. The stock recovered.

So Ford proved the calculation works. Meta proved the market rewards it. Boeing proved that even when hundreds of people die, the math still holds. But all of those are single companies making single decisions. Therefore, the next question is: what happens when it's not one company? What happens when it's the entire culture of the largest bank in America?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created approximately three point five million unauthorized accounts. They signed customers up for credit cards they never applied for. Opened checking accounts in people's names without permission. Generated fake email addresses to enroll customers in online banking without their knowledge. And they did all of this because Wells Fargo's corporate culture demanded cross-selling — employees had quotas to meet, and the pressure was so intense that the only way to survive was to cheat.

Five thousand three hundred employees were fired for doing what management incentivized them to do. The CEO? He retired. With his full benefits.

Total fines: approximately three billion dollars. Wells Fargo's annual revenue: roughly eighty billion. Three billion divided by eighty billion — three point eight percent. Two weeks of revenue for the privilege of defrauding three point five million customers over fourteen years.

The Ponemon Institute published a study showing that the average cost of regulatory compliance for a financial institution is five point four seven million dollars per year. The average cost of non-compliance — when you get caught — is fourteen point eight million. For the average company, it's actually cheaper to comply. But for a company the size of Wells Fargo, the compliance cost is higher and the profit from non-compliance is astronomically higher. They ran the numbers. They made their choice.

Ford, Meta, Boeing, Wells Fargo — each one proved the calculation in a new way. But notice what's still true in all four cases: people went on with their lives. Renters still had apartments. Users still had Facebook. Passengers still flew Boeing. Customers still banked at Wells Fargo. The damage was real, but it was survivable.

Therefore — what happens when the damage isn't survivable? What happens when six hundred thousand people die?

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma, the company that manufactured OxyContin. Internal documents show they knew the drug was highly addictive. They knew people were dying. They funded campaigns to change how doctors thought about pain. They pushed doctors to prescribe higher doses. And they made billions.

More than six hundred thousand Americans have died from opioid overdoses since the crisis began. That's more than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the Sackler family agreed to pay seven point four billion dollars in a settlement with every state in the union. Sounds like a lot. But the Sackler family's estimated personal wealth — the money they pulled out of Purdue before it went bankrupt — is estimated at eleven to thirteen billion dollars. They're paying a portion of their fortune while keeping the rest. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was criminally charged. They filed for bankruptcy through the company, shielded themselves through corporate structure, and emerged with billions intact.

The calculation, stripped of all sentiment: the Sacklers earned roughly thirty-five billion dollars from OxyContin over its lifetime. They're paying back seven point four billion. That's a seventy-nine percent profit margin. On a product that killed six hundred thousand people.

If that's justice, what does injustice look like?

Now zoom out. Because individually, each of these cases looks like a bad company doing a bad thing. But together, they're a system.

RealPage: one point six billion in annual revenue. Penalty: zero dollars. Zero percent.
Meta: fifty-five point eight billion in revenue. Penalty: five billion. Nine percent.
Boeing: fifty-eight point two billion in revenue. Penalty: two point five billion. Four point three percent.
Wells Fargo: eighty billion in revenue. Penalty: three billion. Three point eight percent.
Purdue Pharma: thirty-five billion lifetime revenue from OxyContin. Settlement: seven point four billion. Twenty-one percent — the highest on this list, and it took six hundred thousand deaths and a coalition of fifty states to get there.

Since the year 2000, American corporations have paid over one trillion dollars in fines, penalties, and settlements. One hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid more than one billion dollars during that period. Not once. Repeatedly. And here's the part that should make you angry: those repeat offenders — the companies that get caught again and again — receive smaller fines as a percentage of their revenue than first-time offenders. The system literally rewards recidivism.

The bigger the company, the smaller the fine as a percentage of revenue. The fine is always — always — less than the profit from the violation. The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

And this is where the story turns. Because everything I've just shown you — Ford to Meta to Boeing to Wells Fargo to Purdue — those are cases where the violation already happened, the damage was done, and the fine was assessed after the fact. But what if there's an entire industry right now, today, in 2026, that is breaking the law on an industrial scale, openly, deliberately — because they've watched every company on this list and learned the lesson perfectly?

I'm talking about artificial intelligence. And the calculation has never been more explicit.

As of March 2026, there are ninety active copyright lawsuits against AI companies. Ninety. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company is being sued for the same act: they scraped the entire internet without permission, ingested billions of copyrighted works — books, articles, images, music, code — and used all of it to train models they now sell for billions of dollars.

But here's what makes this different from every other case. In every previous example, the violation was in the past. The company did something wrong, got caught, paid a fraction of their profits. With AI, the violation is the product. The scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract the New York Times from Claude. The training data is inseparable from the product. Which means even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for the data they stole.

In September 2025, Anthropic settled a class action for one point five billion dollars. Authors proved that Anthropic downloaded millions of pirated books from shadow libraries to train their AI. The largest AI copyright recovery in history. But Anthropic's valuation at the time was over sixty billion dollars. The settlement was two point five percent of the company's value. The model trained on those pirated books? Still running.

In January 2026, Universal Music, Concord Music, and ABKCO filed another lawsuit against Anthropic — this one for three point one billion dollars. The lawsuits are stacking up. But the model keeps running. And Anthropic keeps raising money.

OpenAI's situation is even more brazen. The New York Times sued for billions, alleging OpenAI ingested millions of copyrighted articles. A federal judge ordered OpenAI to hand over twenty million anonymized ChatGPT logs as part of discovery — the largest discovery order in AI history. And while the legal process grinds on, OpenAI raised another six point six billion dollars. Their valuation hit a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits aren't slowing them down. They're background noise.

And then there's Perplexity. Reddit created a post that was indexable by Google but otherwise invisible — a trap. Within hours, Perplexity's AI surfaced material from that post. Caught. But after Reddit sent a cease-and-desist letter in May 2024, Perplexity's citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They didn't stop scraping after being told to stop. They scraped more. Because the data is worth more than any fine.

This is the new playbook. Scrape now. Ingest the world's knowledge. Train the model. Make it indispensable. And when the lawsuits come — settle for a fraction of the value you created. The model can't be untrained. The data can't be extracted. The fine is just the price of raw material you took without asking.

It's the Ford playbook, the Meta playbook, the Boeing playbook — but perfected. Because AI companies aren't even pretending anymore. They've watched every other industry prove the math works.

And it's about to get worse. The next generation of AI models — the ones being trained right now — are being trained on you. Not on books from shadow libraries. On your conversations with chatbots. Your emails scanned by AI assistants. Your photos processed by AI editors. Your voice cloned by AI tools you signed up for without reading the terms of service. The data is more personal, more intimate, and more valuable than anything before. And when the lawsuits come — the models will already be trained, the products already on the market, and the settlement a fraction of the profits. Just like every time before.

This is what "move fast and break things" always meant. It never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship the product. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

But it doesn't have to be this way.

The European Union figured it out. GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not four percent of the subsidiary. Not four percent of last quarter. Four percent of everything. Meta's entire business model had to change in Europe. When Ireland's Data Protection Commission levied a one point two billion euro fine against Meta in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error — it was a restructuring event. The math changed, and the behavior followed.

Some of the smartest legal minds in the country have proposed bringing that model here. Fines proportional to revenue, not fixed dollar amounts. Criminal liability for executives — not just the company pays, but the people who made the decision face personal consequences. Disgorgement of profits — you don't just pay a fine, you give back every dollar you earned from the violation. And structural remedies — forced breakups, consent decrees with teeth, industry bans for repeat offenders.

None of this is radical. It's just math. If the penalty exceeds the profit, the calculation changes. If executives face prison, the decision changes. If the fine is four percent of global revenue every year until compliance, instead of a one-time payment negotiated down over five years, the incentive changes.

But until that happens — until the math changes — every company in the world will keep doing exactly what Ford did in 1977, what Meta did in 2019, what Boeing did when three hundred and forty-six people were in the air, what the Sacklers did while six hundred thousand people died, and what every AI company is doing right now as you listen to this.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.
