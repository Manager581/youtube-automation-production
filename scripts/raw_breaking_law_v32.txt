Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge was extraordinary: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing with each other — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a single cent. No financial penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for renters who paid inflated prices for years.

In Dallas, a woman named Marquita Davis watched her rent jump thirty percent in a single year — from twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She couldn't afford it. She moved her two kids into her mother's living room. She wasn't alone. Across the country, renters were being squeezed by the same invisible hand, and none of them knew it.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? What kind of system produces that outcome?

Here's what I found. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. Will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how each version made the next one inevitable, and why the newest version might be the one we can never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula starts with a memo that should have killed a company.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap, popular compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. The car caught fire. People burned to death. Ford's engineers found the flaw before the car went to market. And then someone in the boardroom did the math.

The fix costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. The lawsuits from people burning to death? Only forty-nine point five million. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — pulled directly from the government's own guidelines. The memo recommended against the fix.

People burned to death — but when families sued, the payouts totaled less than the fix would have cost. A criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was eventually recalled. But the lesson had already landed.

In 1977, this was a genuine scandal. Front-page news. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. And it should have been the end — because even though the formula worked financially, it destroyed Ford's reputation. For decades, every executive understood there was a cost the spreadsheet couldn't capture. You might survive the fine. But you'd never survive the front page.

So the calculation had a weakness: public shame. Which created a dangerous question — what if the front page didn't matter?

Forty-two years later, that's exactly what happened.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012 — that's a parole deal with the government, a binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica — a company that harvested personal profiles to build voter manipulation tools — paid for access to eighty-seven million Facebook users' personal data. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To make that real: if you earn a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. But here's the punchline — the stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing.

Think about that. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally allowed to break any rule you wanted for a year? Would you speed everywhere? Skip your taxes? Park in handicapped spots? The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing — and that's exactly what Facebook concluded too. They just didn't ask permission.

Somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability. On the trading floor? Champagne. You cannot write satire this good.

Ford invented the formula, but Ford paid in shame. The Pinto was a cautionary tale. So Meta took Ford's playbook, ran it on eighty-seven million people's data, and proved something Ford never imagined — that the market would actually reward you for getting caught. And because Wall Street celebrated Meta's five-billion-dollar fine as a win, the next company knew there were no real consequences left. Except maybe one. Does the formula survive when people actually die?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS. A single sensor measured the nose angle. If it thought the nose was too high, MCAS forced it down. When the sensor failed, the system shoved the nose toward the ground and the pilots couldn't override it.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four years old, on her way to Kenya to work with a health NGO. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who built consumer safety law in America. She died on Ethiopian 302. Her family sued. And in court, they proved Boeing knew.

Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." Fixing it meant re-certification — having regulators verify the plane was safe. Cost: five hundred million to one billion. So Boeing hid the flaw. Shipped the plane.

Boeing's annual revenue was fifty-eight point two billion dollars. So when the DOJ settled for two and a half billion — with a victim fund of five hundred million split among three hundred and forty-six families — it amounted to sixteen days of revenue. No executive went to prison. And because no executive went to prison, Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still got contracts. Stock recovered.

Death didn't break the math. Samya Stumo was twenty-four, and the system that killed her treated her death as a rounding error.

Two and a half billion for three hundred and forty-six lives barely made the news. But here's the thing that actually kept me up at night — it wasn't the math. It was the fact that in every case so far, someone chose. Ford, Meta, Boeing — a specific person in a specific boardroom decided the profit was worth the risk. Which means someone could have chosen differently. So the playbook needs a villain, right?

What if it doesn't?

Wells Fargo set impossible sales quotas. Because the quotas were impossible, employees had to cheat to keep their jobs. Because employees cheated, three point five million unauthorized accounts were created — credit cards nobody applied for, checking accounts opened without consent. A woman in Florida found out she had a Wells Fargo card she never signed up for when a debt collector called her at work. Because five thousand three hundred employees were doing this, the fraud generated billions. Because the fraud generated billions, the three-billion-dollar fine amounted to two weeks of income on eighty billion in revenue — cheaper than fixing the quotas. So the quotas stayed. The workers got fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

No villain. No memo. Just a machine that made cheating the rational choice — and a fine so small it was cheaper than honesty.

But there's one more question — the worst one. What happens when someone uses the formula deliberately, for decades, and the body count reaches six hundred thousand?

In Kermit, West Virginia — a town of four hundred people — a single pharmacy filled twelve million hydrocodone pills in three years. Four hundred people. Twelve million pills. That's where this story starts. Not in a boardroom. In a small town where the pills just kept coming.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. They knew OxyContin was highly addictive — internal documents prove it. But instead of pulling back, they hired respected physicians to tell other doctors that addiction fears were overblown. Dr. Russell Portenoy, once called the King of Pain, spent years making that case. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." By then, millions were hooked. More than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses.

In 2025, the family agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth — extracted before Purdue went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. About twelve thousand dollars per death. In 1977, Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand. The math got cheaper.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

But every case so far has one thing in common: the violation came first, the fine came second. What if an entire industry studied this playbook and concluded — why wait? What if you broke the law on purpose, before the first product shipped?

That's artificial intelligence. Ninety active lawsuits as of March 2026.

Now, you might think — ninety lawsuits, the courts will fix this. Just sue them and make them pay. Here's why that won't work. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. But with AI, the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a stolen book from Claude. Even if every company loses every case, they keep the models. The fine doesn't punish the theft — it becomes the purchase price, paid after the fact, for data already used.

That's why Anthropic settled a class action for one point five billion in September 2025, watched it amount to two point five percent of their valuation, and kept the model running. That's why OpenAI raised six point six billion in new funding while being sued by the New York Times. The lawsuits aren't obstacles. They're overhead. And the next generation of models is being trained on something more personal — your conversations with chatbots, your emails, your voice. When those lawsuits come, the models will already be built.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law.

So is it over? Is the math unbeatable? No. Because someone already broke it.

The European Union passed a law called GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — and it does one thing differently: fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta changed how it operates across all of Europe — overnight. Not because Mark Zuckerberg had a moral awakening. Because four percent made the violations more expensive than the profits for the first time in the company's history.

That's all it took. Proportional fines. Criminal liability for the executives who sign off. And disgorgement — that's the legal term for forcing a company to hand back every dollar it earned from the violation. Not pay a fine on top. Hand it back. The EU proved that when you change the math, the behavior changes overnight.

But until the math changes here, the formula keeps running. Marquita Davis is still at her mother's house. Her kids still share a room. The algorithm is still setting her rent. And nobody paid anything.

"If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class." That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
