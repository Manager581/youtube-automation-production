# ── ACT 1: WHAT THE ALGORITHM DID (1:00–4:00) ──────────────────────────────

Start with the product itself.

YieldStar is a revenue management platform. Landlords pay a subscription, hand over their rent rolls, their occupancy data, their move-outs, their renewals — everything they know about the units they own. In return, YieldStar tells them what to charge. Unit by unit. Day by day.

On its own, that is nothing new. Hotels have priced rooms this way for thirty years. Airlines do the same thing with seats.

But apartments are not seats. You do not choose to rent an apartment tonight because Wednesdays are off-peak. You rent because your current lease is ending, your job is here, your kid's school is here, and moving costs an average of twelve hundred dollars even if nothing breaks.

A renter is not a shopper. A renter is a hostage with ninety days' notice.

[BEAT]

What RealPage added to the formula was the thing that made it illegal.

The algorithm did not just price one landlord's units. It priced across landlords. To use YieldStar, you had to share your own data with the pool. Your rents, your specials, your vacancies — all of it, fed into a single model that then told you, and your competitors, what everyone should charge.

If you were a single landlord with a YieldStar subscription, you were not making a pricing decision. You were accepting one.

If you were in a market where every major landlord used it — and by 2022, in some cities, that was most of them — the market was no longer a market. It was a shared spreadsheet.

Price fixing is not a complicated crime. Two companies that are supposed to compete agree, directly or indirectly, to set prices together. It has been illegal in the United States since the Sherman Antitrust Act of 1890. It is one of the oldest named white-collar crimes in the country.

The prosecutor investigating RealPage did not have to invent a new category of harm. He had to prove that the old one was still happening.

Now the math.

RealPage has been in the rental pricing business since the early 2000s. YieldStar specifically became the dominant product after RealPage acquired it from LePage Partners in 2017. By the early 2020s, independent investigations — most notably by ProPublica — estimated that YieldStar or its sister products priced somewhere between sixteen and twenty percent of all large multi-family apartments in the United States.

In certain metro areas, the concentration was much higher. In Seattle, an estimated seventy percent of apartments in some neighborhoods ran through the system. In Denver, similar. In parts of Phoenix — where the attorney general's office first flagged the pattern — the number pushed past eighty percent.

Translate that into rent.

Economists looking at YieldStar-saturated markets have consistently found a rent premium that ranges, depending on the study, from about three percent at the low end to nine percent at the high end, relative to comparable non-algorithmic markets.

Take the midpoint. Five percent.

On ten million apartments — the most widely cited figure for YieldStar's footprint at peak — a five percent rent premium is, conservatively, billions of dollars a year. Not across a decade. Every year.

# ── ACT 2.5: WHY THIS MATH WAS ALWAYS GOING TO HAPPEN (7:30–9:30) ──────────

Federal antitrust law has two big enforcement tools. One is civil damages — private parties sue and the defendant pays. The other is criminal prosecution — the Department of Justice charges executives and they face prison.

Criminal prosecution for price fixing is rare. It used to be common — in the 1960s, the government put real executives in real prison cells for fixing real prices. It stopped being common around 1980, and it has been declining in aggressiveness ever since. In 2021 alone, the DOJ closed the smallest number of criminal antitrust cases in the agency's modern history.

Civil damages, meanwhile, have a ceiling that tracks the violator's revenue — not the harm. A firm that makes twenty billion dollars a year can face damages scaled to that revenue. A firm that makes three hundred million dollars a year — which is roughly RealPage's scale — faces a much smaller ceiling, even if the harm it enabled was in the tens of billions.

The cost of the crime is set by what the company made from it. The fine is set by what the company can afford to pay.

[BEAT]

# ── ACT 3: THE ONE THING THAT CHANGED IT (9:30–11:30) ──────────────────────

There is one counter-example, and it is worth sitting with.

In 2023, the European Union fined Meta — the company that owns Facebook — one point two billion euros for a privacy violation. The fine was calculated under the GDPR framework, which scales penalties as a percentage of global annual revenue. Not a fixed cap. Not a negotiated ceiling. A percentage.

Within weeks of that fine, Meta — the same company that had spent over a decade treating U.S. privacy enforcement as a cost of doing business — rewrote its entire European data transfer architecture. They shut down a pipeline they had operated for years. They rebuilt the whole system.

They did it because, for the first time, the number on the fine side of the ledger was bigger than the number on the continue-operating side.

The formula broke. In one jurisdiction. Because one variable changed. Not the severity of the violation. Not the public outrage. The math.

[BEAT]

This is what an effective fine looks like. It is not a number designed to register moral seriousness. It is a number designed to make the next executive meeting about the same behavior end differently.

The United States has never had that number. Not for housing. Not for rent algorithms. Not for antitrust. The fines are calibrated to be survivable. They are calibrated to be a line item.

That is not a bug. That is the design.

[BEAT]
