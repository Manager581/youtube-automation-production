Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge was extraordinary: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing with each other — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a single cent. No financial penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for renters who paid inflated prices for years.

In Dallas, a woman named Marquita Davis watched her rent jump thirty percent in a single year — from twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She couldn't afford it. She moved her two kids into her mother's living room. She wasn't alone. Across the country, renters were being squeezed by the same invisible hand, and none of them knew it.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? What kind of system produces that outcome?

Here's what I found when I started pulling the thread. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. A formula so simple it fits in one sentence: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's the entire operating system.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how each version made the next one possible, and why the newest version might be the one we can never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. And it starts with a memo that should have killed a company.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap, popular compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death. Ford's engineers identified the flaw before the car ever went to market.

Imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers say there's a fix. It costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to retrofit every Pinto on the road. But the lawsuits from people burning to death? Only forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This was a real document, presented in a real courtroom. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number pulled directly from the government's own guidelines. They used the government's math to justify letting their customers burn.

And it worked exactly as predicted. People died. Families sued. But the settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was recalled. But the calculation had proven itself.

In 1977, this was a genuine scandal. A corporation putting a dollar value on human life was shocking. You'd expect the system to have closed this loophole. But it didn't. Because the lesson wasn't "don't kill people." The lesson was: the formula works. Run the numbers first.

But Ford's formula had a weakness. It was reactive — do the crime, pay the fine, absorb the reputational damage. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. So the next company to use the formula had to solve a new problem: what if the market doesn't punish you at all?

Forty-two years later, Meta answered that question.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012 — basically a parole deal with the government, a binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica paid for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million Facebook users without their knowledge. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To make that real: if you earn a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. But here's the punchline — the stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing.

Think about that for a second. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally allowed to break any rule you wanted for a year? Would you speed everywhere? Skip your taxes? Park in handicapped spots? The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing — and that's exactly what Facebook concluded too. They just didn't ask permission.

So Ford proved the formula works, but the market punished Ford's reputation. Meta proved something worse: the market will reward you for getting caught. And every company in America noticed. Which meant the next test was inevitable — if the market won't punish you and regulators can't stop you, does the formula survive when people actually die?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that question.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. All one hundred and eighty-nine people on board died. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four years old, on her way to Kenya to work with a health NGO. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who built consumer safety law in America. She died on Ethiopian 302. Her family sued. And in court, they proved what everyone suspected: Boeing knew.

Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." Fixing it meant re-certification — having federal regulators independently verify the plane was safe. Cost: five hundred million to one billion. So Boeing hid the flaw. Shipped the plane. And MCAS killed three hundred and forty-six people.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion. Two hundred and forty-three million in criminal penalties. One point seven seven billion to airline customers — not victims' families, the airlines. Five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison.

Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. The cost of three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue. Then Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered. Death didn't break the formula.

Ford normalized the calculation. Meta proved markets reward it. Boeing proved even mass death doesn't stop it. Each case made the next one thinkable. But all three were still decisions — one company, one boardroom, someone chose. So here's a genuinely different question: what happens when the formula becomes so embedded that thousands of people commit fraud simultaneously, and nobody thinks they're the problem?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Five thousand three hundred employees — because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. Management built the machine. Fraud was the output. When it came out, five thousand three hundred workers were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Annual revenue: eighty billion. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud. Wells Fargo got caught, paid the fine, got caught again, paid another fine, got caught a third time. Still the fourth-largest bank in America. The punishment isn't a deterrent. It's a subscription fee.

But none of these cases answers the darkest version of the question. I want to slow down here, because what comes next is different.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain — paying respected physicians like Dr. Russell Portenoy, once called the King of Pain, to tell their peers that fewer than one percent of patients would become addicted. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." By then, millions were hooked.

The Sacklers pushed higher doses. They made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the family agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their estimated personal wealth — extracted before Purdue went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. Thirty-five billion in lifetime revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. About twelve thousand dollars per death. In 1977, Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper.

Ford. Meta. Boeing. Wells Fargo. The Sacklers. Five decades. Every fine was less than the profit. Every company survived. Every executive went home. Since 2000, corporations have paid over one trillion dollars in penalties. And repeat offenders receive smaller fines as a percentage of revenue than first-timers. The system rewards doing it again — the legal term is recidivism, and it's supposed to make your punishment worse, not better.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

But every case so far has one thing in common: the violation happened first, the fine came after. What if an entire industry studied this fifty-year playbook and decided to break the law as a deliberate business strategy — before the first product even shipped?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

As of March 2026, ninety active copyright lawsuits target AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — all sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, and selling models trained on stolen material for billions.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. Even if every company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

In September 2025, Anthropic settled a class action for one point five billion. Authors proved Anthropic downloaded millions of pirated books to train their AI. The largest AI copyright recovery in history. But Anthropic's valuation was over sixty billion. Two point five percent. The model? Still running.

OpenAI's situation is more brazen. The New York Times sued for billions. In January 2026, a judge ordered OpenAI to hand over twenty million anonymized ChatGPT logs. While the legal process grinds on, OpenAI raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

And Perplexity — Reddit set a trap. Created a post visible only to Google's crawler. Within hours, Perplexity surfaced it. Caught red-handed. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop. Because the data is worth more than any fine.

Scrape now. Train the model. Settle later. The model can't be untrained.

And the next generation is being trained on you. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails scanned by AI assistants. Your voice cloned by tools you signed up for without reading the terms. When the lawsuits come, the models will already be trained. The settlement will be a fraction of the profits. Just like every time before.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law.

So — does it have to be this way? No. The EU proved it doesn't. GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue — not a subsidiary, everything worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta restructured operations across all of Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because four percent annually made privacy violations unprofitable for the first time. Proportional fines, criminal liability for executives, disgorgement — meaning you return every dollar earned from the violation, not just pay a penalty. When the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks.

Until then, every company runs the same math Ford wrote in 1977. They break the law. They make their money. They pay the fine. Because right now, breaking the law is profitable.

"If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class." That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
