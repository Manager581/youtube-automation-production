Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge was extraordinary: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing with each other — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company generating one point six billion dollars in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a single cent. No financial penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for the renters who paid inflated prices for years.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? Not a reduced fine. Not a slap on the wrist. Nothing. What kind of system produces that outcome?

Here's what I found when I started pulling the thread. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. A formula so simple it fits in one sentence: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's the entire operating system.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how it was tested, how it survived even when hundreds of people died — and why the newest version, happening right now, might be the one we can never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. And it starts with a memo that should have killed a company.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap, popular compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank was positioned so that it ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. People burned to death inside their vehicles. Ford's engineers identified the flaw before the car ever went to market.

Now imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers have just told you there's a fix. It costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to retrofit every Pinto on the road. But the lawsuits from people burning to death? The wrongful death settlements, the burn injury payouts, the vehicle replacement costs? Those will only total forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This wasn't rumor or speculation. This was a real document, presented in a real courtroom. Ford's internal cost-benefit analysis explicitly valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number they pulled directly from the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration's own guidelines. They used the government's math to justify letting their customers burn.

And it worked exactly as the memo predicted. People died. Families sued. But the settlements cost less than the fix would have. A criminal trial in Indiana — one of the only times in American history that a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was eventually recalled. But the calculation had already proven itself.

In 1977, this was a genuine scandal. Front-page news. A corporation putting a dollar value on human life was shocking. So you'd expect the system to have closed this loophole by now. But it didn't. Because the lesson corporate America learned was not "don't kill people." The lesson was: the formula works. Run the numbers first. And that raised a dangerous question — if you can get away with it when people die, what can't you get away with?

Forty-two years later, someone tested that question. And the answer was breathtaking.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission — the government agency responsible for protecting American consumers — announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. The target was Meta, then called Facebook. Here's what happened: in 2012, after getting caught deceiving users about their privacy, Facebook signed what's called a consent decree — essentially a parole deal with the government, a legally binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica — a company that harvested personal profiles to build voter manipulation tools — paid for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million Facebook users without their knowledge. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good. They violated their own parole.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic. Record-breaking. A message to every company in America.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To put that in terms you can feel: if you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. But the real punchline is bigger. The stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. Seven billion. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing at all.

Somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability. On the trading floor? Champagne. You genuinely can't write satire this good. Reality beat you to it.

Now — Ford proved the formula works, but Ford was at least punished reputationally. People were outraged. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. So Meta raised the stakes: what if you use the formula and nobody even cares? What if the market applauds you for getting caught? Meta didn't just survive punishment. It profited from it. And every company in America noticed.

Which raises the next question — one that should genuinely scare you. The formula survived money. It survived privacy violations. It survived public outrage. But does it survive death? Is there a moral cost high enough to break the math?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that question.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. All one hundred and eighty-nine people on board were killed. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS, short for Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System, that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor reading.

Boeing knew. Their engineers flagged the MCAS problem before the plane was certified. Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." But here's the calculation that matters: fixing it properly meant re-certification — the process where federal regulators independently verify that a plane is safe to fly. Cost estimate: five hundred million to one billion dollars. So instead of going back to the FAA, Boeing hid the flaw. They shipped the plane. And because the fix was never made, MCAS killed one hundred and eighty-nine people — and then, five months later, killed one hundred and fifty-seven more.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion dollars. Two hundred and forty-three million in criminal penalties. One point seven seven billion to airline customers — not the victims' families, the airlines. Five hundred million for a victim fund. No executive went to prison.

Boeing's annual revenue, even in a pandemic year: fifty-eight point two billion. That's a hundred and fifty-nine million dollars per day. The cost of three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue.

Then Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still one of only two companies that build commercial aircraft. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

The formula held. Death didn't break it.

And after all this — the Pinto memo, two planes falling out of the sky, the five-billion-dollar fine that made Facebook richer — do you know what the system changed? The press releases got longer. Congress held hearings where senators asked tough questions and then broke for lunch. The regulatory apparatus built this elaborate performance of accountability — serious faces, record-breaking numbers, stern language — that looks exactly like justice and does absolutely nothing. It's theater with a trillion-dollar budget and a standing ovation from Wall Street every single time.

I almost admire it. Almost.

But the formula was still, in each case, a decision. One company. One boardroom. Someone chose. So here's a different question: what happens when nobody chooses? When the formula becomes so embedded in an institution that five thousand three hundred people commit fraud simultaneously, and not one of them thinks they're the problem?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created approximately three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Fake email addresses used to enroll people in online banking without their knowledge. Not a handful of rogue employees. Five thousand three hundred of them — because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. The pressure to hit those quotas was so intense that the only way to keep your job was to cheat. Management built the machine. Fraud was the output.

When it all came out, five thousand three hundred workers were fired for doing what the culture demanded. The CEO? Retired. With his full benefits.

Three billion dollars in fines. Annual revenue: roughly eighty billion. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of systematic fraud against three point five million people.

And here's what makes this even more frustrating. The Ponemon Institute published a study showing that the average cost of regulatory compliance for a financial institution is five point four seven million dollars per year. The average cost of non-compliance — when you get caught — is fourteen point eight million. For the average company, it's actually cheaper to follow the law. But for a company the size of Wells Fargo, the compliance cost is higher — and the profit from non-compliance is astronomically higher. They ran the numbers. They made their choice. The formula, again.

But none of these cases — not one — answers the darkest version of the question. And I want to slow down here, because what comes next is different. It's not about stock prices or planes or fake bank accounts. It's about a family. A quiet, wealthy, deeply private family who understood the formula better than anyone on this list — and used it to kill more Americans than every other case I've mentioned combined.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They knew people were dying. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain. They pushed doctors to prescribe higher doses. And they made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore. Gone.

In 2025, the Sackler family agreed to pay seven point four billion dollars in a settlement with every state in the union. Their estimated personal wealth — money they extracted from Purdue before it went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was criminally charged. They shielded themselves through corporate structure and emerged with billions intact.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin on a product that killed six hundred thousand people. In 1977, Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars. The Sacklers paid about twelve thousand per death. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper.

Now zoom out. Because individually, each of these cases looks like a bad company doing a bad thing. But together, they tell a different story.

RealPage: one point six billion dollars in annual revenue. Penalty: zero dollars. Zero percent.
Meta: fifty-five point eight billion in revenue. Penalty: five billion. Nine percent.
Boeing: fifty-eight point two billion in revenue. Penalty: two point five billion. Four point three percent.
Wells Fargo: eighty billion in revenue. Penalty: three billion. Three point eight percent.
Purdue Pharma: thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Settlement: seven point four billion. Twenty-one percent — the highest on this list, and it took six hundred thousand deaths and a coalition of fifty states to get there.

The pattern is undeniable. The bigger the company, the smaller the fine as a percentage of revenue. And across the board, the fine is always — always — less than the profit from the violation.

Since 2000, American corporations have paid over one trillion dollars in fines, penalties, and settlements. One hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid more than a billion dollars during that period. Not once. Repeatedly. And here's the part that should make you angry: those repeat offenders — the companies that get caught again and again — they receive smaller fines as a percentage of their revenue than first-time offenders. The system literally rewards recidivism. The more you break the law, the less you pay.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Now — every case I've shown you has one thing in common. The violation happened first. The fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta harvested the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid. But what if an entire industry studied this fifty-year playbook, did the math in advance, and decided to break the law as a deliberate business strategy — before the first product even shipped?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

As of March 2026, there are ninety active copyright lawsuits against AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company is being sued for the same fundamental act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works — books, articles, images, music, code — and using all of it to train models they now sell for billions of dollars.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a newspaper from Claude. Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

Let me show you how explicit the math is.

In September 2025, Anthropic — the company behind the AI assistant Claude — settled a class action for one point five billion dollars. Authors proved that Anthropic had downloaded millions of pirated books from shadow libraries to train their AI. The settlement was the largest AI copyright recovery in history. But Anthropic's valuation at the time was over sixty billion dollars. The settlement was two point five percent of the company's value. The model trained on those pirated books? Still running. Every conversation with Claude draws on that training data.

And then, in January 2026, Universal Music, Concord Music, and ABKCO filed another lawsuit against Anthropic. This one for three point one billion dollars. The lawsuits are stacking up. But the model keeps running. And Anthropic keeps raising money.

OpenAI's situation is even more brazen. The New York Times sued for billions, alleging OpenAI ingested millions of their copyrighted articles. In January 2026, a federal judge ordered OpenAI to hand over twenty million anonymized ChatGPT logs as part of discovery — the largest discovery order in AI history. And while the legal process grinds on — a process that could take years — OpenAI raised another six point six billion dollars in funding. Their valuation hit a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits aren't slowing them down. They're background noise.

And then there's Perplexity. Reddit set a trap — they created a post that was visible to Google's search engine but otherwise hidden. Within hours, Perplexity's AI surfaced material from that post. Caught. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist letter. Perplexity's response? Their citations to Reddit increased forty-fold. They didn't stop scraping after being told to stop. They scraped more. Because they did the math. The data is worth more than any fine.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later for a fraction. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the cost of raw material taken without asking.

And it's about to get worse. The next generation of AI models — the ones being trained right now, in 2026 — are being trained on you. Not on books from shadow libraries or articles from the New York Times. On your conversations with chatbots. Your emails scanned by AI assistants. Your photos processed by AI editors. Your voice cloned by AI tools you signed up for without reading the terms of service. The data these companies are collecting right now is more personal, more intimate, and more valuable than anything that came before. And when the lawsuits come — and they will come — the models will already be trained. The products will already be on the market. And the settlement will be a fraction of the profits. Just like every time before.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship the product. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

So — does it have to be this way?

No. And we know this because someone already proved it doesn't.

The European Union's GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Not one quarter. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error — it was a restructuring event. Meta changed how they operate across all of Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because the math changed. And the behavior followed.

Proportional fines work. Criminal liability for executives works — not just the company writing a check, but the person who signed off facing prison. Disgorgement of profits works — you don't just pay a fine, you return every dollar earned from the violation. Structural remedies work — forced breakups, enforceable consent decrees, industry bans for repeat offenders.

None of this is radical. It's just math. If the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. If executives face prison, the decision changes. If the fine is four percent of global revenue every year until compliance — instead of a one-time payment negotiated down over five years — the incentive flips.

The EU proved it. Meta's entire business model had to change in Europe. Not because Mark Zuckerberg had a moral awakening. Because four percent of global revenue, annually, made privacy violations unprofitable for the first time in the company's history. The formula broke — and behavior changed overnight.

But until the math changes, every company will keep running the same formula Ford wrote in 1977 — the one Meta validated, Boeing tested with human lives, Wells Fargo industrialized, the Sacklers pushed to its ultimate extreme, and every AI company is perfecting right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

There's a comment that went viral under a video about this phenomenon. It got five hundred likes in a few hours, and it said something that captures this entire system in a single sentence: "If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class."

That's not cynicism. That's the operating system. And every company on this list is running it.

But here's what I think is the most dangerous part of all of this. Not the fines. Not the deaths. Not the stolen data. It's that the system is getting better at being broken. The fines are getting more sophisticated — not bigger relative to the crimes, but more precisely calibrated to be just painful enough that the public thinks justice was served, and just small enough that the company's business model is never threatened. A five-billion-dollar fine sounds devastating until you learn it's nine percent of one year's revenue. A two-and-a-half-billion-dollar settlement sounds serious until you learn it's sixteen days. A seven-point-four-billion-dollar opioid settlement sounds historic until you learn the family kept most of their fortune.

The fines aren't failing. They're performing exactly as designed. They create the appearance of accountability without the substance.
