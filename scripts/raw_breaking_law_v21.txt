Two days ago, something happened that should change how you think about the law. A company called RealPage settled a federal lawsuit with the Department of Justice. The charge was extraordinary: RealPage built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing with each other — and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the algorithm's recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep. For years.

The settlement? Zero dollars.

A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a single cent. No financial penalty. No admission of guilt. No compensation for renters who paid inflated prices for years.

In Dallas, a woman named Marquita Davis watched her rent jump thirty percent in a single year — from twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She couldn't afford it. She moved her two kids into her mother's living room. She wasn't alone. Across the country, renters were being squeezed by the same invisible hand, and none of them knew it.

RealPage is still operating today. The algorithm is still running. And your rent is still high.

But how? How does a company get caught fixing rent for ten million apartments and pay nothing? What kind of system produces that outcome?

Here's what I found. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. A formula so simple it fits in one sentence: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed. That's the entire operating system.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how each version made the next one inevitable, and why the newest version might be the one we can never undo.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. And it starts with a memo that should have killed a company.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap, popular compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. The car caught fire. People burned to death. Ford's engineers identified the flaw before the car ever went to market.

Imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers say there's a fix. It costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. But the lawsuits from people burning to death? Only forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. The memo recommended against the fix.

This was a real document, presented in a real courtroom. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number pulled directly from the government's own guidelines. They used the government's math to justify letting their customers burn.

And it worked as predicted. People died. Families sued. The settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial — one of the only times a corporation was tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The Pinto was recalled. But the calculation had proven itself.

In 1977, this was a genuine scandal. Front-page news. The Pinto became a symbol of corporate evil. And that should have been the end — because even though the formula worked financially, it destroyed Ford's reputation. For decades, every executive in America understood there was a reputational cost. You might survive the fine, but you'd never survive the front page.

That created the next question: what if you could use the formula and the market didn't punish you? What if it rewarded you?

Because that's exactly what happened — forty-two years later.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission announced the largest privacy penalty in history. Five billion dollars. Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012 — that's essentially a parole deal with the government, a binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica paid for access to eighty-seven million Facebook users' personal data. Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up. Not down. Up.

The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. To make that real: if you earn a hundred thousand dollars a year, that's a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. But here's the real punchline — the stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. The punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing.

Think about that. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally allowed to break any rule you wanted for a year? Would you speed everywhere? Skip your taxes? Park in handicapped spots? The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing — and that's exactly what Facebook concluded too. They just didn't ask permission.

And this is where the formula evolved into something new. Because Ford got punished in public — the Pinto became a cautionary tale. But Meta showed every boardroom in America that the cautionary tale was outdated. You can get caught, pay the fine, and come out richer. Wall Street doesn't price fines as failure. It prices them as overhead. And once that message landed, the only question left was the one nobody wanted to ask: if the market rewards you for breaking the law, and regulators can't stop you — does the formula survive when people actually die?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that question.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 crashed six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw — a system called MCAS. Here's what it did: a single sensor on the outside of the plane measured the angle of the nose. If the sensor thought the nose was too high, MCAS automatically forced it down. The problem was that the sensor could fail. And when it failed, MCAS shoved the nose toward the ground and the pilots couldn't override it.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four years old, on her way to Kenya to work with a health NGO. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who built consumer safety law in America. She died on Ethiopian 302. Her family sued. And in court, they proved what everyone suspected: Boeing knew.

Internal messages show employees calling it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." But fixing it meant re-certification — having federal regulators independently verify the plane was safe. Cost: five hundred million to one billion. So Boeing hid the flaw. Shipped the plane. And MCAS killed three hundred and forty-six people.

The DOJ settlement: two and a half billion. Criminal penalties: two hundred and forty-three million. A victim fund of five hundred million — split among three hundred and forty-six families. No executive went to prison.

Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. The cost of three hundred and forty-six deaths? Sixteen days of revenue. Boeing then violated the settlement itself. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

Death didn't break the formula. Not even close.

But every case so far was still a decision. One company, one boardroom, someone chose. So here's where it gets genuinely unsettling: what happens when the formula becomes the culture itself — when thousands of people commit fraud simultaneously, and nobody thinks they're the problem?

Here's one number that will tell you: three point five million.

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Five thousand three hundred employees — because the quotas management set were impossible to meet honestly. Management built the machine. Fraud was the output. When it came out, the workers were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Annual revenue: eighty billion. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of fraud. Wells Fargo got caught, paid, got caught again, paid again, got caught a third time. The government put them under what's called an asset cap — it prevents the bank from growing larger. Not smaller. Just not larger. That's the ceiling of corporate punishment in America. Wells Fargo is still the fourth-largest bank in the country. The punishment isn't a deterrent. It's a subscription fee.

I want to slow down here, because what comes next is different. It's not about stock prices or planes or fake bank accounts. It's about a family who understood the formula better than anyone — and used it to kill more Americans than every other case I've mentioned combined.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain — paying respected physicians like Dr. Russell Portenoy, once called the King of Pain, to tell their peers that fewer than one percent of patients would become addicted. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." By then, millions were hooked.

The Sacklers pushed higher doses and made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore.

In 2025, the family agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth — extracted before Purdue went bankrupt — is eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. Thirty-five billion in lifetime revenue. About twelve thousand dollars per death. In 1977, Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Now — every case so far shares one thing: the violation happened first, the fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta harvested the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid. But what if an entire industry studied this fifty-year playbook, did the math in advance, and decided to break the law before the first product shipped — not as a side effect, but as the business model itself?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

As of March 2026, ninety active copyright lawsuits target AI companies. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney — all sued for the same act: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, and selling models trained on stolen material for billions.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model weights. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a newspaper from Claude. Even if every company loses every lawsuit, they keep the models. The fine becomes the purchase price for data they stole.

Sarah Silverman was one of the first to sue. She and two other authors proved OpenAI ingested their copyrighted books without permission. In September 2025, Anthropic settled a separate class action for one point five billion after authors proved the company downloaded millions of pirated books to train their AI. Largest AI copyright recovery in history. But Anthropic's valuation was over sixty billion. Two point five percent. The model? Still running.

Meanwhile, OpenAI raised another six point six billion while being sued by the New York Times for billions. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise. And the next generation of models is being trained on something more personal — your conversations with chatbots, your emails scanned by AI assistants, your voice cloned by tools you signed up for without reading the terms. When those lawsuits come, the models will already be trained. The settlement will be a fraction of the profits.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law.

So — does it have to be this way? No. The EU proved it doesn't. They passed a law called GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — and it does one simple thing differently: fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Not one quarter. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta restructured operations across all of Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because four percent annually made privacy violations unprofitable for the first time. The math changed. And the behavior followed overnight.

Proportional fines. Criminal liability for the executives who sign off. Disgorgement — that means you don't just pay a penalty, you return every dollar earned from the violation. When the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. It's not complicated. It's just math.

But until the math changes, every company runs the same formula Ford wrote in 1977. They break the law. They make their money. They pay the fine. Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

"If the penalty for a crime is a fine, then the law only exists for the lower class." That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
