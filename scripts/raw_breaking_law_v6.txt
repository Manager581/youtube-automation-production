Two days ago, a company you've probably never heard of settled one of the most consequential lawsuits in American history. And the outcome should terrify you. Not because of what they did — but because of what it tells us about how the system actually works.

The company is called RealPage. The DOJ proved they built an algorithm that coordinated rent prices across more than ten million apartments in the United States. Not suggested prices — coordinated them. Their software took confidential data from competing landlords and used it to tell every one of them how much to charge. Ninety percent followed the recommendations. Rents went up. Across the country. In lockstep.

The settlement was zero dollars. Not reduced. Not negotiated down. Zero.

A company making one point six billion a year, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away without paying a cent. The algorithm is still running. Your rent is still high.

How is that possible? What kind of system looks at rent-fixing for ten million apartments and says — no penalty?

Here's what I discovered when I started pulling the thread. RealPage isn't an outlier. It's the latest expression of a formula that corporate America has been perfecting since 1977. A formula so simple it fits in one sentence: will the fine be less than the profit? If yes, proceed.

I'm going to show you where this formula was born, how it was tested, how it survived even when people died — and how, right now, it's being used in a way that might be permanently irreversible.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula has an origin story. And it starts with a memo.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto — a cheap compact car — had a problem. The fuel tank was positioned so it ruptured in rear-end collisions. When it ruptured, the car caught fire. Ford's engineers knew about this before the car went to market.

Now imagine you're in that boardroom. Your engineers have just told you there's a fix. It costs one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars to retrofit every Pinto on the road. But the lawsuits from people burning to death? Those will only cost forty-nine point five million.

One hundred and thirty-seven million to fix it. Forty-nine million to let people die. Ford chose the forty-nine million.

This wasn't speculation. This was a real memo, presented in a real court. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number they took directly from the government's own guidelines. They used Washington's math to justify letting their customers burn.

The result? Exactly what the memo predicted. People died. Families sued. But the settlements cost less than the fix. A criminal trial — one of the only times a corporation has been tried for reckless homicide — ended in acquittal. The formula worked.

And here's the thing that matters most: in 1977, this was a scandal. It was on the front page. A corporation putting a dollar value on human life was genuinely shocking. But the system didn't close the loophole. It studied it. And forty-two years later, someone figured out how to make the formula even more profitable.

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission — the agency that's supposed to protect consumers — announced the largest privacy fine in history. Five billion dollars. Meta — then called Facebook — had signed a consent decree in 2012. That's a legally binding promise to the government, in this case a promise to stop deceiving users about how their data was shared. They broke it. A political consulting firm called Cambridge Analytica had been paying for access to the personal data of eighty-seven million users without their knowledge, and Facebook allowed it because the money was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic.

On the same day, Facebook's stock price went up.

If you make a hundred thousand dollars a year, this fine is a nine-thousand-dollar speeding ticket. For doing something that made you billions. The fine wasn't a punishment. It was a price tag — the exact cost of violating your privacy. And the price was reasonable.

But here's where this tips from infuriating into genuinely, darkly comic. The stock gained seven billion in market cap that day. Seven billion. The fine was five billion. Which means the punishment made the company worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing. Let me say that again, because I need you to actually hear it. The government punished Facebook, and Facebook got richer. An FTC commissioner was writing a press release about "holding corporations accountable" while, on the trading floor, someone was probably ordering lunch on a company card to celebrate.

This is what the formula looks like when it matures. Ford proved the math works. Meta proved the market will applaud you for it. And Wall Street took notes.

Now I need to shift gears. Because everything so far has been about money — data, privacy, stock prices. But what happens when the formula meets a situation where the stakes aren't abstract? Where the cost isn't privacy or rent, but three hundred and forty-six human lives?

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. One hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 — six minutes after takeoff. One hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane. Same flaw. A system called MCAS — Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System — that forced the nose down based on a single faulty sensor reading.

Boeing's engineers knew. They flagged the problem before the plane was certified. Internal messages called it a plane "designed by clowns, who in turn are supervised by monkeys." But fixing it properly meant re-certification — the process where federal regulators verify a plane is safe to fly. Cost: five hundred million to one billion dollars. So rather than go back to the FAA, Boeing buried the flaw. They shipped the plane. And because the flaw was never fixed, it killed one hundred and eighty-nine people — and then, five months later, killed one hundred and fifty-seven more.

The settlement: two and a half billion. Mostly paid to airlines, not families. No executive went to prison. Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion — a hundred and fifty-nine million per day. Three hundred and forty-six lives cost them sixteen days of revenue.

Then Boeing violated the terms of the settlement itself. They broke the deal about the law they broke that killed those people. Still one of two companies that build commercial aircraft. Still getting government contracts. Stock recovered.

Ford proved the formula works. Meta proved the market rewards it. Boeing proved it holds even when people die. But here's what all three have in common — in each case, the formula was a choice made by executives in a boardroom. A deliberate decision.

So what happens when the formula stops being a decision and becomes a culture? When it's not the CEO choosing — it's five thousand three hundred employees, all doing the same thing, because the system demands it?

Between 2002 and 2016, Wells Fargo employees created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Not three point five million dollars — three point five million fake accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without permission. Fake emails enrolling people in services they never requested. Five thousand three hundred employees committed fraud — not because they were criminals, but because the quotas were impossible to meet honestly. Management built a machine, and fraud was the output.

When it came out, five thousand three hundred workers were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits.

Three billion in fines. Eighty billion in annual revenue. Three point eight percent. Two weeks of income for fourteen years of systematic fraud.

I want to stop here for a moment. Because I realize something is happening to you right now, and I want to name it.

You're going numb.

Four cases. Each one worse. The numbers keep getting bigger and the consequences keep getting smaller. At some point, your brain just checks out. Another billion. Another CEO who walked away fine. It starts to feel like weather — something that just happens.

That response is the system working as designed. When outrage never turns into structural change, outrage becomes free PR. The company waits out the news cycle, and the formula holds.

So I need you to stay with me for one more. Because this one is different. This is where the formula stops being about money or negligence or even culture — and becomes about something that doesn't have a word.

The Sackler family controlled Purdue Pharma. They knew OxyContin was highly addictive. Internal documents prove they knew people were dying. They funded campaigns to reshape how doctors thought about pain. They pushed higher doses. And they made billions while more than six hundred thousand Americans died from opioid overdoses. More than the population of Baltimore. Gone.

In 2025, the Sacklers agreed to pay seven point four billion. Their personal wealth — extracted from Purdue before it went bankrupt — is estimated at eleven to thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was criminally charged.

Thirty-five billion in lifetime OxyContin revenue. Seven point four billion in settlement. Seventy-nine percent profit margin. On a product that killed six hundred thousand people.

If that's justice — what does injustice look like?

Now let me show you the full picture. Because each of these cases, alone, looks like a single bad actor. Lined up, they're a system.

RealPage: one point six billion in revenue. Penalty: zero.
Meta: fifty-five point eight billion. Penalty: five billion. Nine percent.
Boeing: fifty-eight point two billion. Penalty: two point five billion. Four percent.
Wells Fargo: eighty billion. Penalty: three billion. Four percent.
Purdue: thirty-five billion lifetime. Settlement: seven point four billion. Twenty-one percent — the highest on this list, and it took six hundred thousand deaths and all fifty states to get there.

Since 2000, American corporations have paid one trillion dollars in fines. One hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid over a billion — repeatedly. And repeat offenders pay smaller fines as a percentage of revenue, not larger. The more you break the law, the less it costs.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

But every case I've shown you has one thing in common: the violation happened first, and the fine came after. Ford built the car, then paid. Meta harvested the data, then paid. Boeing shipped the plane, then paid.

What if an entire industry looked at this fifty-year track record, studied the formula, and decided to break the law as a strategy — before the violation even began?

That industry is artificial intelligence. And the formula has reached its final form.

As of March 2026, ninety active copyright lawsuits. OpenAI, Anthropic, Google, Meta, Stability AI, Midjourney, Perplexity — every major AI company sued for the same thing: scraping the entire internet without permission, ingesting billions of copyrighted works, training models worth billions on content they never paid for.

Here's what makes AI the endgame. With Ford, you could recall the car. With Meta, you could delete the data. With Boeing, you could ground the fleet. With AI — the violation is the product. Scraped data is baked into the model. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a newspaper from Claude. Even if every lawsuit succeeds, the models survive. The fine becomes the purchase price for stolen data.

Anthropic settled for one point five billion in September 2025 — largest AI copyright recovery ever — after authors proved they trained on millions of pirated books. Anthropic's valuation: over sixty billion. Settlement: two point five percent. The model? Still running.

January 2026: Universal Music, Concord, and ABKCO filed suit for three point one billion. Lawsuits stack. Model keeps running.

OpenAI: the New York Times sued for billions. A federal judge ordered twenty million ChatGPT logs turned over — largest discovery order in AI history. While the courts crawl forward, OpenAI raised six point six billion more. Valuation: a hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits are background noise.

Perplexity: Reddit built a trap — a post visible to Google, invisible otherwise. Within hours, Perplexity surfaced it. Caught. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist. Perplexity's citations to Reddit went up forty-fold. They scraped more after being told to stop.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later for a fraction. The model can't be untrained. The fine is just the price of raw material taken without asking.

And the next generation — training now — is being trained on you. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails. Your photos. Your voice. Data more personal than anything that came before. When the lawsuits come, the models will already be built.

"Move fast and break things" never meant break software. It meant break the law. Ship it. Apologize later. Pay the fine. Keep the money.

So — does it have to be this way?

No. Someone already proved it doesn't.

The European Union's GDPR fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Not one quarter. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros in 2023, it wasn't a rounding error — it was a restructuring event. Meta changed how they operate across all of Europe. Not because they wanted to. Because the math changed.

Proportional fines work. Criminal liability for executives works — not just the company writing a check, but the person who signed off facing prison. Disgorgement works — returning every dollar earned from the violation. Structural remedies work — breakups, enforceable decrees, industry bans for repeat offenders.

None of this is radical. It's just math. If the penalty exceeds the profit, the formula breaks. If executives face prison, the decision changes. If the fine is four percent of global revenue every year until compliance, the incentive flips.

But until the math changes, every company will keep running the same formula Ford wrote in 1977 — the one Meta validated, Boeing tested with human lives, Wells Fargo industrialized, the Sacklers took to its logical extreme, and every AI company is perfecting right now.

They'll break the law. They'll make their money. And they'll pay the fine.

Because right now, breaking the law is profitable. And until it isn't, nothing changes.

The Pinto memo was written forty-nine years ago. The formula it created hasn't been eliminated. It's been perfected. But the question at its heart hasn't changed once: will the fine be less than the profit?

The only thing that breaks it is math. Not outrage. Not articles. Not videos. Math. When the penalty exceeds the profit, companies stop. Not because they find their conscience. Because they find a new spreadsheet.

We know what works. The question is why we haven't done it.

Now you know the math.
