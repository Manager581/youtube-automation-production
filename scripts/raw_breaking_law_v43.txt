In 2019, Facebook was fined five billion dollars. The largest penalty in FTC history. Eighty-seven million people's data harvested and sold. And on the day the fine was announced, Facebook's stock went up. Not sideways. Up. The market added six billion dollars in value. Mark Zuckerberg personally gained one point one billion dollars. The punishment made the company richer than if the government had done nothing.

That is not a failure of the system. That is the system working exactly as designed.

Because there is a formula. It was invented in 1977, perfected across five decades, and the version running right now — on your data, your posts, your conversations — is the most profitable business strategy ever created. Every company that runs it has discovered the same thing: the fine is cheaper than the profit. And that makes the fine the price of doing business. Not a punishment. A purchase.

Reddit caught an AI company scraping every post ever written. The company's response? They scraped forty times more. Right now, over seventy lawsuits are active against AI companies training on your words, your photos, your books — and not one of them has slowed down.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

[BEAT]

The formula starts with a car on fire.

In 1977, Ford discovered the Pinto's fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions at low speeds. The car would crumple, the tank would puncture, and the occupants would burn alive. This was not speculation. Ford had the crash test data. They knew.

Two engineers in Ford's Environmental and Safety Engineering division — E.S. Grush and C.S. Saunby — wrote a cost-benefit analysis. Not a metaphor. An actual document, with actual numbers, submitted to the National Highway Traffic Safety Administration. Here is what it said.

Fixing every Pinto on the road: eleven dollars per vehicle, times twelve point five million vehicles. One hundred and thirty-seven million dollars.

Expected cost of not fixing: one hundred and eighty burn deaths at two hundred thousand dollars each. One hundred and eighty serious burn injuries at sixty-seven thousand each. Twenty-one hundred burned vehicles at seven hundred each. Total: forty-nine point five million dollars.

They chose forty-nine point five. They shipped the car. People burned to death.

[BEAT]

At least twenty-seven people died. The National Highway Traffic Safety Administration confirmed that number. Other estimates run as high as five hundred. We'll never know the real count because Ford fought every investigation for years.

But here's what happened when they finally got caught.

A thirteen-year-old boy named Richard Grimshaw was a passenger in a Pinto that was rear-ended in San Bernardino, California. The fuel tank ruptured. The car became an inferno. The driver, Lilly Gray, died. Richard survived — barely. He was burned over his entire body. He lost parts of his fingers. He lost his left ear. He underwent dozens of skin grafts over years of surgery.

The jury was so horrified they awarded one hundred and twenty-five million dollars in punitive damages. In 1978, that was unprecedented. The largest punishment a jury had ever leveled at a corporation. A signal that this formula — this cold arithmetic of acceptable death — would not be tolerated.

The judge reduced it to three point five million.

And Ford was acquitted of criminal homicide charges. No executive went to prison. No one was personally fined. The Grush-Saunby memo is still taught in business schools. Not as a warning. As a case study.

[PAUSE]

The lesson every boardroom in America learned from the Pinto was not "don't do this." The lesson was: next time, don't write it down.

The formula didn't die. It evolved. And over the next forty years, every company that used it made the same discovery. The punishment always costs less than the profit. You just had to be smarter about the paper trail.

Wells Fargo proved you didn't even need a villain.

[BEAT]

In the mid-2000s, Wells Fargo adopted an internal mantra: Eight is Great. Every customer should have eight products — checking, savings, credit card, mortgage, insurance, investment. Eight. The number came from the top. It cascaded down through regional managers to branch managers to the tellers behind the counter. And it became a quota.

If you didn't hit your numbers, you got put on a warning list. Then you got demoted. Then you got fired. Five thousand three hundred employees were terminated between 2011 and 2016 for failing to meet sales targets. But here's the thing — the targets were impossible to meet legitimately. No human being walks into a bank and signs up for eight products. So the tellers started creating accounts their customers never asked for. They moved money between accounts. They created fake email addresses. They ordered credit cards that arrived at customers' homes unexplained.

Three point five million unauthorized accounts.

[BEAT]

And nobody decided to commit fraud. That's the genius of the system. There was no memo. No meeting where someone said "let's steal from our customers." There was just a number — eight — and a consequence for not reaching it. The pressure did the rest. The fraud was structural. It emerged from the incentive system the way mold emerges from moisture. No one person was responsible because everyone was responsible, which means no one was.

When it finally broke open, Wells Fargo paid three billion dollars in combined federal penalties. Three billion sounds enormous until you do the math. Wells Fargo's annual revenue was around eighty-six billion. The fine was twelve days of revenue. Twelve days. A parking ticket, scaled up.

John Stumpf, the CEO, resigned. The board clawed back sixty-nine million dollars from him. That sounds like accountability until you learn he could have walked away with two hundred million. He kept a hundred and thirty million in cash, stock, and options. He settled an SEC charge for two point five million and agreed not to serve as a bank officer again. That was his punishment.

Carrie Tolstedt ran the division that created the fake accounts. Her original exit package was one hundred and twenty-four point six million dollars. After clawbacks, she kept roughly fifty-seven million. She was the only Wells Fargo executive criminally charged. She pled guilty to obstruction of a bank examination. Her sentence: three years of probation and six months of home confinement. Zero prison time. The five thousand three hundred tellers at the bottom were fired. The woman at the top served her sentence at home.

[PAUSE]

Ford taught the formula. Wells Fargo refined it. But Purdue Pharma found the final optimization: what if you move the money before they even calculate the fine?

[BEAT]

In 1996, Purdue launched OxyContin. Richard Sackler, the company's president, told his sales force at the launch event — and this is a direct quote from court records — that the launch would be followed by a, quote, blizzard of prescriptions that will bury the competition.

A blizzard.

They marketed OxyContin as less addictive than other painkillers. They sent sales representatives to doctors' offices with studies claiming addiction rates under one percent. Those studies were misleading. Purdue knew the drug was highly addictive. Internal documents showed they tracked the abuse of their own product and used that data to target higher-prescribing physicians. The playbook was simple: find the doctors who prescribe the most, and give them more reasons to prescribe.

In Kermit, West Virginia — a town of four hundred people — a single pharmacy called Sav-Rite received nine million opioid pills in two years. Nine million pills. Four hundred people. That's eleven thousand pills per resident per year. The pharmacy filled between six hundred and a thousand prescriptions every day, over ninety percent of them for hydrocodone. The pharmacist went to prison. The distributors who shipped the pills — McKesson, Cardinal Health, AmerisourceBergen — paid fines. The Sacklers, who created the demand, moved their money.

[BEAT]

And this is the part that separates Purdue from every other case. Before the lawsuits hit, before the bankruptcy filing, the Sackler family withdrew ten point seven billion dollars from the company. Not millions. Billions. An independent audit in December 2019 confirmed the number. Four billion was transferred between 2008 and 2016 alone — years when the family was already discussing litigation exposure in private. Richard Sackler wrote to his sons acknowledging that shareholders — meaning his family — had, quote, taken the money out of the business during years of, quote, massive amounts of cash.

They saw the lawsuits coming a decade in advance. And they moved the money first.

More than eight hundred thousand Americans have died from opioid overdoses since 1999. The settlement, finalized in 2025, totaled seven point four billion dollars. The Sacklers' remaining fortune is estimated between eleven and thirteen billion. If you divide the settlement across the dead, it comes to roughly nine thousand dollars per person. Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand in 1977. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. The price of a life got cheaper.

[PAUSE]

Here's a thought experiment. What if you could pay nine percent of your income and be legally immune for a year? Speed everywhere. Skip your taxes. Park wherever you want. The answer is so obvious it's embarrassing. Of course you would. That's the deal Facebook got. That's the deal every company in this video got. Except nobody asked you first.

Now let me show you what happens when the formula meets the internet.

[PAUSE]

Facebook was the bridge.

In 2013, a Cambridge University researcher named Aleksandr Kogan built a personality quiz app called This Is Your Digital Life. Two hundred and seventy thousand people downloaded it. Harmless, right? Except Facebook's API — the system that lets apps talk to Facebook — gave the quiz access to not just those people's data, but to all of their friends' data too. Every like, every post, every photo, every private message if the permissions were broad enough. Two hundred and seventy thousand downloads became eighty-seven million people's data harvested in weeks.

Facebook knew this was happening. In 2014, they changed the API to close the loophole. But they never enforced deletion of the data already taken. Cambridge Analytica — a political targeting firm — used that data to build psychological profiles of American voters. They were hired by the Trump campaign and by groups supporting Brexit. They didn't just know what you liked. They knew what you feared, what made you angry, what would make you click.

[BEAT]

When the story broke in March 2018, Facebook suspended Cambridge Analytica. Zuckerberg testified before Congress. He said sorry. He looked uncomfortable. The FTC investigated and in July 2019, announced the largest fine in their history: five billion dollars.

Here is what five billion dollars means to Facebook. In 2019, Facebook's revenue was seventy point seven billion. The fine was seven percent. But that's annual revenue — the real comparison is profit. Facebook's net income that year was eighteen point five billion. The fine was about twenty-seven percent of one year's profit. Paid once. For a violation that ran for years and touched eighty-seven million people.

The FTC vote was three to two. Party line. The two dissenting commissioners didn't think the fine was too harsh. They thought it was too lenient. They wanted to sue, not settle. They wanted to hold Zuckerberg personally liable. They lost the vote.

And on July twelfth, when the fine amount first leaked, Facebook's stock rose one percent. The market had expected worse. Investors were relieved. Mark Zuckerberg's personal net worth increased by one point one billion dollars that day. The fine didn't punish Facebook. It reassured Wall Street that the punishment had a ceiling.

[PAUSE]

Five decades. Five industries. Rent fixed by algorithm. Bank accounts opened by quota. Pills pushed by sales targets. Data harvested by API. The fine was always cheaper than the profit.

That was the old formula. Now let me show you the new one.

[BEAT]

In October 2025, a Reddit engineer did something clever. They created a post — an ordinary-looking post about a fictional company — and made it visible only through Google's search index. You couldn't find it by browsing Reddit. You couldn't stumble on it. The only way to reach it was through a search engine crawler. It was bait. A tripwire designed to catch one specific thing: an AI company scraping Reddit's content without permission.

Within hours, Perplexity AI cited the post. Word for word. The trap worked.

Reddit's legal team sent a cease-and-desist in May 2024. Standard procedure — stop scraping our content, or we'll sue. Here's what happened next. Perplexity's citations to Reddit content didn't go down. They went up. Forty times. Not a little more. Forty times more. After being caught. After receiving a formal legal demand to stop.

[BEAT]

Step back. A company is caught stealing. They receive a letter saying stop. Their response is to steal forty times as much.

This isn't arrogance. This is the formula. They calculated that the data was worth more than any fine. That the model being trained on that data was worth more than any lawsuit. And that once the data is inside the model, there is no retrieving it.

Reddit filed a federal lawsuit in October 2025. The defendants weren't just Perplexity — they included proxy companies that masked IP addresses and rotated identities to bypass Reddit's access controls. They allegedly scraped billions of Google search results containing Reddit content.

But Perplexity is just one company. And the playbook is everywhere.

[PAUSE]

Anthropic — the company behind Claude — downloaded seven million copyrighted books from shadow libraries. Not legitimate publishers. Pirate websites. LibGen. The Pirate Library Mirror. Seven million books, scraped and fed into training data without paying the authors a cent.

A federal judge ruled in June 2025 that using legally acquired books for AI training might be fair use. But downloading pirated copies? That's infringement. Anthropic settled for one point five billion dollars in September 2025. The largest copyright settlement in history. It covered four hundred and eighty-two thousand works at roughly three thousand dollars per book.

Anthropic's valuation at the time of settlement: over sixty billion dollars. The fine was two point five percent. The model kept running the next day.

[BEAT]

OpenAI used training datasets they internally called "books one" and "books two." Both were downloaded from LibGen — the same shadow library. OpenAI later deleted the datasets, but the fact of their use became evidence when the New York Times sued them in December 2023.

That case has since been consolidated with sixteen other copyright lawsuits. In January 2026, a federal judge ordered OpenAI to produce twenty million ChatGPT conversation logs as evidence. OpenAI tried to limit the search to keyword queries. The judge said no. Produce the full sample. De-identified, but complete. Twenty million conversations that might show exactly how copyrighted material surfaces in AI outputs.

OpenAI's response to all of this? They raised six point six billion dollars in new funding. Valuation: one hundred and fifty-seven billion. The lawsuits aren't obstacles. They're a line item on the balance sheet.

Seventy-plus copyright lawsuits are active against AI companies as of March 2026. Three judges have ruled on fair use so far. Two found fair use. One found infringement. The next batch of rulings comes this summer. Not one AI company has slowed down.

[BEAT]

Here's the part that separates AI from every case that came before it.

Ford could recall the Pinto. They didn't want to, but they could. Facebook could delete the data. Boeing could ground the fleet. Purdue could stop manufacturing the pills. In every previous version of this formula, the violation was reversible. Expensive, slow, and usually nobody did it voluntarily — but the off switch existed.

With AI, there is no off switch.

When a model trains on scraped data, that data dissolves into the model's parameters like sugar dissolved in water. You cannot un-dissolve it. You cannot identify which outputs came from which inputs. You cannot point to a paragraph and say "that came from the New York Times" or "that came from a Reddit post" or "that came from a children's book published in 2019."

Even if every AI company loses every lawsuit — all seventy-plus of them — they keep the models. The models keep running. The products built on those models keep generating revenue. The fine doesn't undo the training. The fine is just the purchase price of the raw materials, paid after the factory is already built.

Scrape now. Train the model. Make it indispensable. Settle later. The Ford Pinto formula, perfected.

[PAUSE]

If you've ever posted on Reddit, commented on YouTube, written a blog, tweeted anything, left a review, uploaded a photo — your content is in training datasets right now. Not hypothetically. Provably. Common Crawl, the dataset most large language models train on, contains over two hundred and fifty billion web pages. Your words are in there. The companies building hundred-billion-dollar products on your words did not ask your permission. And by the time the legal system catches up, the models will be infrastructure. As embedded in daily life as electricity. Too essential to shut down. Too valuable to unwind.

[BEAT]

And it's already happening to your rent.

RealPage. The company that takes confidential pricing data from competing landlords and feeds it into an algorithm that coordinates rent increases across more than thirteen million apartments. ProPublica broke the story in October 2022. The Department of Justice sued. And in November 2025, they announced a settlement.

Here's what the settlement requires. RealPage has to stop using competitors' nonpublic data. They have to limit model training to data at least twelve months old. They have to remove mechanisms that prop up prices. Landlords have to be able to set rents below the algorithm's recommended floor.

Here's what the settlement does not include. A fine. Compensation to renters. Admission of guilt. The algorithmic rent-fixing tool that affected thirteen million apartments? Still running. Modified, but running. And RealPage responded to the settlement by suing New York State for passing a law banning algorithmic rent-setting.

They got caught fixing rent for millions of people. The penalty was a policy change. And their first move was to sue the state that tried to stop them.

So is the formula unbeatable?

No. Because someone already broke it.

The European Union passed GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — and did one thing differently. Fines scale to four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary's revenue. Not a negotiated number. Everything, worldwide.

Since GDPR took effect in May 2018, regulators have imposed seven point one billion euros in fines. Nine of the ten largest went to tech companies. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros for transferring European user data to the United States, Meta didn't shrug it off. They didn't issue a press release and watch their stock climb. Within weeks, they rewrote their data transfer agreements, shut down a pipeline they'd run for a decade, and rebuilt their European infrastructure from scratch.

The same company that turned a five-billion-dollar American fine into a stock rally rewrote its entire data architecture over a European one. Because for the first time, the violation was more expensive than the profit.

TikTok was fined five hundred and thirty million euros for transferring European user data to China. Amazon was fined eight hundred and eighty-seven million for behavioral advertising without consent. These aren't symbolic. They're structural. Four percent of global revenue means the fine grows with the company. The bigger you are, the more it costs. The formula breaks.

[BEAT]

Proportional fines. Criminal liability for executives — not the tellers at the bottom, not the branch managers following orders, but the people who designed the system. Disgorgement — a legal term that means the company doesn't just pay a penalty. It hands back every dollar earned from the violation. Not a fine on top. The actual money. Returned.

The tools exist. The legal frameworks exist. The question has never been how to fix this. The question is why we haven't. And the answer is the same math that runs everything else in this video. The companies that benefit from the formula fund the campaigns, staff the advisory boards, draft the legislation, and write the rules. The fine stays small because the people who pay it set the ceiling.

[PAUSE]

Every company in this video did the same calculation. The fine will be less than the profit. Ship the car. Open the accounts. Push the pills. Harvest the data. Scrape the internet. Fix the rent. And when they come for you — and they will, eventually — the settlement will be a fraction of what you made. A speeding ticket at corporate scale. A cost of doing business.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it. And the current price is a bargain.

The algorithm that raised your rent is still running. The model trained on your words is still generating revenue. The data harvested from your profile was never deleted. And every company watching this formula work is doing the same math right now.

The question isn't whether they'll break the law. The question is whether you'll make them pay more than it's worth.
