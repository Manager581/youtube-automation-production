Last month, Marquita Davis lost her apartment. Not because she stopped paying rent. Because an algorithm decided she should pay more.

Marquita is a medical assistant in Dallas. Two kids, seven and eleven. She'd lived in the same unit for three years — good tenant, never missed a payment. Then her rent jumped thirty percent in a single year. Twelve hundred to over fifteen hundred dollars a month. Same apartment. Same neighborhood. Same job. She couldn't make it work. She moved her kids into her mother's living room.

Here's what Marquita didn't know. Her landlord didn't raise her rent. A company called RealPage did. RealPage built software that takes confidential pricing data from competing landlords — data those landlords are legally forbidden from sharing — and uses it to coordinate rent increases across more than ten million apartments nationwide. Ninety percent of landlords followed the algorithm's price. The Department of Justice called it price fixing. The settlement? Zero dollars. No fine. No compensation. No admission of guilt.

A company generating one point six billion in annual revenue, owned by a private equity firm that paid ten point two billion to acquire it, walked away free. The algorithm is still running.

But this isn't about one company. This is about a formula — invented in 1977, perfected across five decades, and just reaching a version that might be impossible to reverse.

This is why breaking the law is profitable.

The formula starts with a car on fire.

In 1977, the Ford Pinto had a design flaw. The fuel tank ruptured in rear-end collisions. The car caught fire. People burned to death. Ford's engineers found the flaw before the car went to market. Someone in the boardroom did the math. Fixing every Pinto: one hundred and thirty-seven million dollars. Expected wrongful death lawsuits: forty-nine point five million. Ford valued a human life at two hundred thousand dollars — a number from the government's own safety guidelines — and recommended against the fix.

Families sued. Payouts totaled less than the fix. Indiana tried Ford for reckless homicide — one of the only times a corporation faced that charge. Acquitted. The lesson landed. If the fine is less than the profit, proceed.

The Pinto became a symbol. For decades, every executive understood there was a cost the spreadsheet couldn't capture — public shame. You might survive the lawsuit. You'd never survive the front page.

Marquita Davis wasn't born yet. But the formula that would raise her rent was already written.

For forty years, shame kept the formula in check. It only worked in the shadows. So the next test was simple: could a company break the law in the biggest possible spotlight and come out ahead?

In July 2019, the Federal Trade Commission fined Facebook five billion dollars — the largest privacy penalty in history. Facebook had signed a consent decree in 2012, which is essentially parole for corporations — a binding promise to stop sharing user data without permission. They broke it. Cambridge Analytica harvested eighty-seven million users' personal data to build voter manipulation tools. Facebook allowed it because the revenue was too good.

Five billion dollars. The FTC called it historic. Congress held hearings. Editorials called it a watershed moment for accountability.

The same day the fine was announced, Facebook's stock went up. Not down. Up. The stock gained seven billion in market cap — the punishment made Facebook worth two billion more than if the government had done nothing. The fine was nine percent of one year's revenue. Somewhere in Washington, an FTC commissioner was drafting a press release about accountability. On Wall Street, traders looked at nine percent and saw a bargain.

Ford invented the formula but paid in shame. Meta ran the same playbook on eighty-seven million people and proved something new — that the market would reward you for getting caught. Marquita's data was in that Cambridge Analytica harvest. She just didn't know it yet.

The formula survived shame. Does it survive death?

Three hundred and forty-six people are about to answer that.

On October 29th, 2018, Lion Air Flight 610 crashed into the Java Sea thirteen minutes after takeoff. A hundred and eighty-nine dead. Five months later, Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 went down six minutes after takeoff. A hundred and fifty-seven dead. Same plane — the 737 MAX. Same flaw — a system called MCAS, the Maneuvering Characteristics Augmentation System. A single sensor measured the nose angle. If it failed, the system shoved the nose toward the ground and pilots couldn't override it.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four years old, heading to Kenya to work in global health. Her great-uncle was Ralph Nader — the man who built consumer safety law in America. She died on Ethiopian 302.

In court, her family proved Boeing knew. Internal messages: "designed by clowns." Fixing MCAS meant re-certification — having the FAA verify the entire plane from scratch. Cost: five hundred million to a billion dollars. Boeing hid the flaw.

The DOJ settled for two and a half billion. Victim fund: five hundred million split among three hundred and forty-six families. Boeing's annual revenue: fifty-eight point two billion. The fine was sixteen days of income. No executive went to prison. Boeing violated the settlement itself. Still got contracts. Stock recovered.

Samya Stumo was twenty-four. The system that killed her treated her death as a rounding error. Two and a half billion for three hundred and forty-six lives. That's seven point two million per person — except most of that went to airlines, not families. The victim fund worked out to roughly one point four million per family. For a company worth over a hundred billion dollars.

Now — every case so far shares something. Ford had a memo. Meta had executives who chose profit over privacy. Boeing had engineers overruled by the boardroom. In each case, you can point at someone and say: you chose this. Someone could have chosen differently.

The formula needs a villain. Right?

What if it doesn't?

This is the part that scared me. Because everything up to now has a face you can blame. What comes next doesn't.

Wells Fargo set sales quotas — eight financial products per customer. Checking, savings, credit cards, home equity, insurance. The number was nearly impossible to hit honestly, because most customers don't need eight products. But the quotas were tied to your performance review, your bonus, and whether you kept your job.

Two choices: miss quotas and get fired, or cheat.

Start with a branch manager in Florida — call her Sandra. Twelve employees. Corporate sends weekly rankings by product numbers. Sandra's branch is in the bottom quartile. Her district manager calls. He doesn't say cheat. He says the numbers need to improve, and branches that underperform get restructured. Sandra tells her team.

A teller named Marcus opens a savings account for a customer who came in to deposit a check. The customer doesn't know. Marcus doesn't feel great about it. But he watched two coworkers get fired last month for low numbers, and he has student loans. The account generates a small fee. Marcus hits his target. Sandra's branch moves up. The district manager doesn't call this week.

Now multiply that by five thousand three hundred employees. Over several years, they created three point five million unauthorized accounts. Credit cards nobody applied for. Checking accounts opened without consent. A retired teacher named Gloria found out she had a Wells Fargo credit card when a debt collector called. She'd never applied for anything.

Marquita Davis probably had one too. At that time, she banked at Wells Fargo. Millions of customers did. Most never checked.

Here's what makes Wells Fargo different from everything else. At Ford, someone wrote a memo. At Boeing, someone hid a flaw. Those are decisions made by identifiable people. At Wells Fargo, there was no memo. No villain. The quota system was the engine. Each branch manager was a rational actor — push the numbers or lose your job. Each teller below them was a rational actor — fabricate accounts or get replaced. Each executive above was a rational actor — the revenue looks great, don't ask how.

Nobody decided to create three point five million fake accounts. The system decided. And that's the scariest version of the formula — because you can punish a person. You can't punish a system. When no individual made the decision, the punishment has no target.

Think about what that means for every company in every industry. You don't need a villain. You don't need a conspiracy. You just need a set of incentives where cheating is the rational choice at every level of the organization. The fraud emerges on its own, like mold in a warm room. And when it's discovered, there's nobody to arrest — because everybody was just doing their job.

Wells Fargo paid three billion in fines. Revenue: eighty billion. Two weeks of income. Five thousand three hundred employees at the bottom of the machine were fired. The CEO retired with full benefits. The stock recovered within a year.

The Ford Pinto had a villain — a memo with names on it. Wells Fargo didn't need one. The system was the villain. And you can't fine a system into behaving.

So the formula works without shame, without a villain, and through three hundred and forty-six deaths. But there's one more test. What happens when someone runs it deliberately for decades, and the body count reaches six hundred thousand?

In Kermit, West Virginia — population four hundred — a single pharmacy received twelve million hydrocodone pills in three years. Twelve million. For four hundred people. That's thirty thousand pills per resident. Enough to give every man, woman, and child in Kermit a dose every few hours for three straight years.

The Sackler family owned Purdue Pharma. Internal documents prove they knew OxyContin was highly addictive. Instead of pulling back, they hired physicians to tell other doctors the addiction fears were overblown. Dr. Russell Portenoy spent years making that case. He later admitted his claims were "without evidence." By then, millions were hooked. More than six hundred thousand Americans have died from opioid overdoses.

Before Purdue declared bankruptcy, the Sacklers systematically transferred billions in personal wealth out of the company. They saw the lawsuits coming. They moved the money first. The 2025 settlement: seven point four billion — structured to preserve most of their fortune, which sits between eleven and thirteen billion. No Sackler went to prison. No Sackler was charged.

Twelve thousand dollars per death. Ford valued a life at two hundred thousand in 1977. The formula didn't just survive fifty years. It got cheaper per death, per decade, per version.

In Dallas, Marquita's mother fills prescriptions at the same pharmacy chain that distributed those pills. The formula doesn't care which industry it runs in.

The law doesn't prevent corporate crime. It prices it.

Now. Every case I've shown you has one structural feature in common — and it's the feature that's about to break.

The violation happened first. The fine came second. You could recall the car. Delete the data. Ground the fleet. Pull the pills. The harm was always — at least in theory — reversible.

So what happens when it isn't? What happens when the violation and the product are the same thing — when you couldn't return the stolen goods even if a court ordered you to?

In October 2025, Reddit set a trap. They created a post — indexed by Google but otherwise inaccessible — and waited to see if Perplexity AI would scrape it. Perplexity did. Reddit sent a cease-and-desist letter. Perplexity's response? Their citations to Reddit content increased forty times. They scraped more after being told to stop. Because the data was worth more than any fine would ever be.

That one incident tells you everything about where the formula has arrived. Ninety active lawsuits as of March 2026. Every major AI company is being sued for the same act — training models on data they never had permission to use.

Here's why this is different from everything before. With Ford, you recall the car. With Meta, you delete the data. With Boeing, you ground the fleet. With Purdue, you pull the pills. But with AI, the violation is the product. Scraped data — books, journalism, music, images, your conversations — is woven into the model during training. You can't un-train GPT. You can't extract a stolen book from Claude. The stolen goods are dissolved into the product, like sugar into water. You can't unstir it.

The fine doesn't punish the theft. It becomes the purchase price — paid after the fact — for data already consumed. Every AI company studied fifty years of corporate penalties and concluded, as a deliberate business strategy, that the optimal move is to take everything first and settle later.

Anthropic settled for one point five billion in September 2025 — two point five percent of their valuation. The model kept running the next day. OpenAI raised six point six billion in new funding while actively being sued by the New York Times. A judge ordered them to hand over twenty million ChatGPT logs. The investors didn't blink.

The lawsuits aren't obstacles. They're overhead.

And the next generation of models is being trained on something more personal than books. Your conversations with chatbots. Your emails processed by AI assistants. Your voice, fed into transcription services that promise privacy while feeding the machine. When those lawsuits come, the models will already be built. The purchase will already be complete.

"Move fast and break things" was never about breaking software. It was always about breaking the law. And unlike every previous version of the formula, this one can't be recalled, deleted, grounded, or pulled from shelves. It's permanent. The formula has reached its final form.

Right now, Marquita Davis is using a customer service chatbot to dispute her rent. That conversation is training data. The model it feeds will optimize pricing for the next generation of rental algorithms. The fine will come later. The model will already exist. The rent will already be set.

So is the formula unbeatable?

No. Because someone already broke it.

The European Union passed GDPR — the General Data Protection Regulation — and it does one thing differently. Fines are four percent of global annual revenue. Not a subsidiary. Everything, worldwide. When Ireland fined Meta one point two billion euros, Meta restructured its entire European data operation overnight. Not from moral awakening. Because four percent made the violation more expensive than the profit — for the first time in the company's history.

Three things. That's all it took. Proportional fines — scaled to global revenue, not negotiated down. Criminal liability for the executives who sign off — not the five thousand three hundred employees at the bottom. And disgorgement, the legal term for forcing a company to return every dollar earned from the violation. Not pay a fine on top. Hand it back. All of it.

The EU proved that when you change the math, the behavior changes overnight. So why hasn't the United States done the same thing?

Because the formula has lobbyists. In 2024, the ten most penalized US corporations spent four hundred and twelve million dollars on lobbying — more than the GDP of some countries. The formula funds campaigns. It sits on advisory boards. It shapes the legislation meant to constrain it. Every attempt to bring GDPR-style proportional fines to the US has died in committee — killed by the same companies that would be affected by it. The math won't change because the people who benefit from the math are the ones writing the rules.

That's the real reason this keeps happening. Not because the solution is complicated. Because the solution is opposed by the most powerful institutions on earth.

Since 2000, American corporations have paid over one trillion dollars in penalties. A hundred and twenty-seven companies have each paid over a billion. The most penalized companies are repeat offenders. And repeat offenders receive smaller fines as a percentage of revenue — the system rewards recidivism, which is the legal term for doing it again.

Marquita Davis is still in her mother's living room. Her kids still share a room. The algorithm that raised her rent is still running. RealPage paid nothing. And somewhere right now, an AI model is training on her data — her rental history, her credit score, her conversations — building the next version of the system that displaced her family.

If the penalty for a crime is a fine, the law only exists for the lower class. That's not cynicism. That's arithmetic.
