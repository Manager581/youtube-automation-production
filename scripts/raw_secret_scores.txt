

Mary Louis paid her rent on time for sixteen years. Sixteen years. Not a single missed payment, not a single late check, not once. She was, by every reasonable measure, the kind of tenant that any landlord should want. So when she applied for a new apartment, she expected what anyone would expect — approval. Instead, she got a rejection letter. No real explanation. No human being she could call to ask why. Just a form that said, essentially, no. And the thing that denied her wasn't a person. It wasn't a landlord who looked at her application and made a judgment call. It was a number. A score she had never seen, generated by a company she had never heard of, using data she had never been asked to verify. Mary Louis had a secret score. And so do you.

Right now, as you go about your day, there are numbers attached to your name that you have never seen. Not your credit score — you probably know about that one. These are different. These scores predict whether you'll pay your rent. Whether you'll be a good employee. Whether you're likely to commit a crime. Whether your insurance claim is legitimate. Whether you deserve medical care. They are calculated in the dark, by algorithms you cannot examine, using data you did not provide, and they are shaping your life in ways you cannot see. The companies that build these scores have names you've probably never encountered. But they know you. They know you very, very well.

Let's start with the one that got Mary Louis. The company is called SafeRent, formerly known as CoreLogic. Their product is a tenant screening score — a single number that tells a landlord whether you're worth the risk. Sounds reasonable on the surface. Landlords need to make decisions. But here's the problem. SafeRent's algorithm doesn't just look at your rental history. It pulls from an enormous web of data — court records, eviction filings, debt records — and it crunches all of that into a score. A score that, according to a federal lawsuit, systematically discriminated against Black and Hispanic renters. SafeRent settled that lawsuit for two point two seven five million dollars. They didn't admit wrongdoing. They never do. But the Department of Justice found that their algorithm produced scores that disproportionately harmed people of color, effectively encoding racial discrimination into a math equation and calling it objective.

So think about what happened to Mary Louis. Sixteen years of perfect behavior, overridden by a machine. And she's not unusual. She's typical. Because the entire point of these systems is to remove human judgment from the equation. That's how they're sold — as neutral, as unbiased, as purely mathematical. But mathematics is not neutral when the data it feeds on is poisoned. And the data is poisoned. It has been poisoned for decades, by a society that has never treated everyone equally. When you train an algorithm on historical data from an unequal world, you don't get fairness. You get inequality at scale. You get discrimination that moves at the speed of a computer, that processes thousands of applications per hour, that never gets tired, never feels guilty, and never has to look anyone in the eye.

Now here's the part that should make your stomach drop. Only three percent of renters know which company screened them. Three percent. That means ninety-seven out of every hundred people who get rejected by one of these algorithms don't even know who to be angry at. They don't know what data was used. They don't know what the score was. They don't know how to challenge it. They just know they didn't get the apartment. And they move on, carrying a secret mark they cannot see, applying to the next place, getting rejected again, never understanding why. It is Kafka's "The Trial" made real, except it's not fiction, and it's not happening to one person. It's happening to millions.

But tenant screening is just one corner of this world. Let's talk about what happens when you apply for a job. Because the same logic — the same faith in algorithms, the same removal of human judgment, the same black-box scoring — has consumed the hiring process at hundreds of major companies. You've probably experienced it. You submit your resume online. You wait. You hear nothing. You assume a human read your application and decided you weren't qualified. But increasingly, no human read anything. Your resume was parsed by software, scored by an algorithm, and discarded before a person ever saw your name. Companies like HireVue took this even further. They asked candidates to record video interviews — not with a person, but with a camera. Then artificial intelligence analyzed your facial expressions, your word choice, your tone of voice, and generated a score. Your employability, reduced to the way your mouth moved when you answered a question about your greatest weakness. Pymetrics did something similar with neuroscience-based games — short cognitive tests that claimed to measure your potential through pattern recognition and reaction time. The future of hiring, they called it.

Derek Mobley knows what that future looks like. Derek is a Black man who applied to over a hundred and fifty jobs. He didn't just apply casually — he was systematic, persistent, desperate to find work. And he was rejected from every single one. Automatically. Without a human ever reviewing his application. At one point, records show he submitted an application at one fifty in the morning. That's not someone who isn't trying. That's someone who can't sleep because the system keeps saying no. Derek filed a lawsuit alleging that the AI screening tools used by these companies discriminated against him because of his race. And he's not wrong to suspect that. A study by the Brookings Institution found that when identical resumes were submitted to AI hiring tools, white-associated names were preferred eighty-five point one percent of the time. Eighty-five percent. That's not a bug in the system. That is the system.

Think about what that means for a moment. If you have a name that sounds white, the machine smiles at you more than eight times out of ten. If you don't, you're fighting uphill before a single human has read a word you've written. And the companies that sell these tools will tell you the algorithm is race-blind, that it doesn't see color. But it doesn't need to see color. It sees zip codes. It sees the names of schools. It sees gaps in employment that correlate with incarceration, which correlates with race, which correlates with poverty, which correlates with everything else in a society that was built on inequality. The algorithm doesn't need to be racist. It just needs to be accurate — accurate to a world that already is.

Now let me tell you about Kyle Behm, because his story is the one that haunts me. Kyle was a young man who struggled with bipolar disorder. He was in treatment. He was managing his condition. He was trying to do what everyone tells you to do — get back out there, get a job, move forward with your life. So he applied for entry-level positions. Hourly work. The kind of jobs that are supposed to be the bottom rung of the ladder, the place where everyone can get a start. But every application included a personality test. A long, probing questionnaire that asked about his moods, his feelings, his mental state — questions that, for someone with a mental health condition, were essentially a trap. Kyle failed every single one. Not because he couldn't do the work. Not because he wouldn't show up on time. But because an algorithm decided that his personality profile — shaped by a condition he was actively treating — made him a risk. The rejections piled up. The sense of worthlessness grew. And Kyle Behm attempted suicide.

His father, a lawyer, investigated. He discovered that the personality tests were manufactured by a company called Kronos, and that they were being used by major retailers — companies you shop at every week — to screen out applicants at the front door. Not based on skills. Not based on experience. Based on their answers to questions that functioned, in practice, as a mental health screening. Which, under the Americans with Disabilities Act, is illegal. Kyle's father filed complaints with the Equal Employment Opportunity Commission. But here's the thing about fighting these systems. The companies have armies of lawyers. They have studies they funded themselves that say the tests are valid. They have decades of selling these tools to HR departments that don't understand the science but love the efficiency. One family against that machine — it's David and Goliath, except Goliath has a legal team and David is recovering from a suicide attempt.

Let me show you how deep the data goes. LexisNexis — you might recognize that name from legal databases — operates one of the largest consumer data operations in the world. They maintain eighty-three billion records on two hundred and eighty-two million people. Let me say that again. Eighty-three billion records. On a population of roughly three hundred and thirty million. That's over two hundred and fifty data points for every man, woman, and child in America. They know where you've lived, who you've lived with, what cars you've driven, what court cases you've been involved in, what properties you've owned, what debts you've carried. They compile all of this into products they sell to landlords, to employers, to insurance companies, to law enforcement. And you have almost certainly never been told what they have on you.

This data doesn't just sit in a file somewhere. It moves. It flows. It gets fed into algorithms that make decisions about your life. And here's where it gets really dangerous — the data is often wrong. Court records are full of errors. People with similar names get their files merged. Charges that were dismissed still show up as if you were convicted. Debts that were paid off still appear as outstanding. And the algorithms that consume this data don't know the difference. They can't know the difference. They just see the numbers.

Carmen Arroyo found this out the hard way. Carmen's son was in an institution — a fact that appeared in screening databases tied to her name. When she applied for housing, that information surfaced. A charge that had been dismissed — not reduced, not pled down, fully dismissed — still appeared in her background check as if it were an active concern. Her housing application was denied. Not because of anything she had done. Not because of any threat she posed. But because a database carried a ghost of an accusation that no longer existed in the eyes of the law, but very much existed in the eyes of the algorithm. Carmen Arroyo was being punished for something that, legally, never happened.

You might be thinking — okay, but surely there's oversight. Surely someone is watching these companies, making sure the scores are accurate, making sure they're not discriminating, making sure people have the right to see what's being said about them. And the answer is — barely. The regulatory landscape is a patchwork of half-measures. New York City passed Local Law 144, which requires companies to audit their AI hiring tools for bias. That sounds good until you learn that the audits are often conducted by firms the companies hire themselves, that the results are sometimes so vague as to be meaningless, and that the enforcement mechanism has no teeth. Illinois passed the Biometric Information Privacy Act — BIPA — which gives people the right to sue when their biometric data is collected without consent. That's how some of the lawsuits against HireVue-style video analysis tools gained traction. Colorado passed an AI Act that requires disclosures and impact assessments for high-risk AI systems. But these are three states and one city in a country of fifty states and thousands of jurisdictions. The vast majority of Americans have no legal protection against algorithmic discrimination. None.

And the money behind these systems is staggering. RealPage — a company that provides software to landlords across the country — was acquired for nine point six billion dollars. Nine point six billion. That's how much the market values the ability to set rents, screen tenants, and manage properties using algorithms. The Department of Justice later filed an antitrust lawsuit against RealPage, alleging that its software was being used by competing landlords to coordinate pricing — essentially, algorithmic collusion. Landlords who were supposed to be competing with each other were all feeding their data into the same system, and the system was telling all of them to raise rents. Not because of market forces. Not because of supply and demand. Because an algorithm said so, and everyone trusted the algorithm.

This is the world we've built. A world where the most consequential decisions in your life — where you live, where you work, what healthcare you receive, how much you pay — are increasingly made not by people but by systems that no one fully understands and almost no one is watching. And these systems are not neutral observers. They are products. They are built to be sold. Their customers are not you. Their customers are landlords who want to minimize risk, employers who want to cut costs, insurance companies who want to deny claims. Your interests and the algorithm's interests are not the same.

Consider what happened at Cigna, one of the largest health insurance companies in the country. An investigation revealed that Cigna used an automated system to deny claims — and in a single month, that system denied over sixty thousand claims. Sixty thousand. One system. One month. These weren't complex cases that required careful medical judgment. They were processed in bulk, at speed, by a machine that was optimized not for patient care but for the company's bottom line. Sixty thousand people were told that their medical treatment — treatment their doctors had prescribed, treatment they needed — would not be covered. And most of them, faced with the complexity and exhaustion of the appeals process, simply gave up. The algorithm was counting on that. The system works not because it's right, but because fighting it is so hard that most people don't bother.

This is the fundamental asymmetry at the heart of the secret score economy. The companies that build these systems have unlimited resources, unlimited data, and unlimited patience. You have a rejection letter you don't understand and a phone number that goes to voicemail. They operate at scale — millions of decisions per day. You are one person, trying to figure out why you were denied an apartment, or a job, or a medical procedure. The system is designed so that even when it's wrong — and it is wrong, routinely — the cost of being wrong falls on you, not on them. When SafeRent wrongly rejects a qualified tenant, SafeRent doesn't lose anything. The tenant loses the apartment. When an AI hiring tool wrongly screens out a qualified candidate, the company just moves on to the next applicant. The candidate is the one who sits up at one fifty in the morning, submitting another application, wondering what's wrong with them.

And that's perhaps the cruelest part of all this. These systems don't just deny you opportunities. They make you blame yourself. When you don't know there's a score, when you don't know there's an algorithm, when you don't know the game is rigged — you internalize the rejection. You think you're not good enough. You think you interviewed poorly. You think your application was weak. You think there's something wrong with you. And there isn't. There's something wrong with the system. But the system will never tell you that, because the system doesn't know you exist. You are a row in a spreadsheet, a probability in a model, a risk score that either clears the threshold or doesn't. The machine doesn't care about your sixteen years of perfect rent. The machine doesn't care about your hundred and fifty applications. The machine doesn't care that you're recovering from a suicide attempt and just need someone to give you a chance. The machine has no concept of a chance. It has thresholds, and you either meet them or you don't.

So what can be done? The honest answer is that we are at the very beginning of figuring that out. The laws being passed — in New York, in Illinois, in Colorado — are first attempts, early drafts of a regulatory framework that doesn't yet exist at the scale it needs to. Europe is further ahead, with its AI Act imposing broader requirements. But even there, enforcement is an open question. The technology moves faster than the law, and the companies deploying these systems have every incentive to move fast and ask forgiveness later.

But there are things you can do. You can request your consumer files from companies like LexisNexis, SafeRent, and CoreLogic. Under the Fair Credit Reporting Act, they are legally required to show you what they have. Most people never ask. Ask. You can dispute errors — and you should expect to find errors, because at the scale these companies operate, errors are not the exception but the norm. You can support legislation that requires algorithmic transparency, that gives people the right to know when an AI system has made a decision about them, and the right to challenge that decision with a human being who has the power to override it. You can demand that the companies making billions off your data be held accountable when that data is wrong, when their algorithms discriminate, when their systems cause harm.

Because here's the thing about secret scores. They only work in the dark. They rely on your ignorance. They rely on you not knowing who SafeRent is, not knowing what LexisNexis has, not knowing that the job application you spent two hours on was rejected by a machine before a human ever saw it. The moment you know, the dynamic changes. Not completely. Not overnight. But fundamentally. Because a system that cannot survive scrutiny is a system that does not deserve your trust.

Mary Louis was a good tenant for sixteen years. Derek Mobley applied to a hundred and fifty jobs. Kyle Behm just wanted a chance to work. Carmen Arroyo was haunted by a charge that no longer existed. None of them knew about the scores. None of them knew about the algorithms. None of them knew who to fight or how to fight them. They were invisible to the systems that judged them, and the systems were invisible to them.

You are being scored right now. By companies you have never heard of, using data you never gave them, feeding algorithms you will never see. Those scores are deciding where you can live, where you can work, what medical care you can receive, and how much you will pay for the roof over your head. They are doing this at scale, at speed, and in the dark. And the single most dangerous thing about all of it — the thing that makes the whole machine work — is that until this moment, you didn't know.

Now you do. The question is what you're going to do about it. Because those scores aren't going away. The companies that build them are growing. The data they collect is expanding. The algorithms are being deployed in more industries, in more decisions, in more corners of your life. Every year, the web gets tighter. Every year, the scores get harder to escape. And every year, fewer decisions about your life are made by human beings who can look at you, hear your story, and use their judgment. That world is disappearing. It's being replaced by a world of thresholds and probabilities, of risk models and automated denials, of secret numbers that follow you from apartment to apartment, from job to job, from doctor to doctor, like a shadow you cannot see and cannot outrun.

The only thing that has ever changed a system this entrenched is people who refused to accept it. People who asked questions the system was designed to make them never think to ask. People who demanded to see the number. People who said — you don't get to score me in the dark. You don't get to reduce my life to a probability. You don't get to use data I never gave you to make decisions I never consented to. You don't get to do this in secret.

The scores are real. The harm is real. And the silence around all of it — that long, profitable silence — is the thing that needs to end first.
