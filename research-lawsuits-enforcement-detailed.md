## 1. TENANT SCREENING CASES

### (A) Louis v. SafeRent Solutions -- LANDMARK ALGORITHMIC DISCRIMINATION

- **Full name**: Louis, et al. v. SafeRent Solutions, et al.
- **Case No.**: 1:22-cv-10800
- **Court**: U.S. District Court, District of Massachusetts
- **Judge**: Hon. Angel Kelley
- **Filed**: May 25, 2022
- **Settlement agreement executed**: March 28, 2024
- **Final approval**: November 20, 2024
- **Plaintiffs**: Two Black women housing voucher holders; lead plaintiff Mary Louis had 16 years of on-time rent payments
- **Defendants**: SafeRent Solutions, LLC and Metropolitan Management Group, LLC
- **Counsel for plaintiffs**: Cohen Milstein, National Consumer Law Center (NCLC), Greater Boston Legal Services
- **DOJ involvement**: Civil Rights Division filed a statement of interest supporting the plaintiffs

**Allegations**:
SafeRent's "Registry ScorePLUS" algorithm (score range 200-800) relied heavily on credit history and eviction data but **failed to consider housing voucher subsidies** -- even though vouchers guarantee over 73% of rent is paid directly by a public housing authority to the landlord. As a result, voucher holders, particularly Black and Hispanic applicants, received disproportionately lower scores and faced higher rejection rates.

**Settlement terms -- $2.275 million total**:
- Monetary compensation to class members
- SafeRent **barred from issuing approve/decline recommendations** for voucher holders unless the algorithm is validated by an independent third-party organization agreed to by plaintiffs
- Must conduct "more robust evaluation" of prospective voucher tenants based on their full record rather than relying on algorithmic score alone
- If SafeRent develops any new tenant screening score, it must be **third-party validated** for 5 years
- Required to evaluate housing voucher tenants' eligibility based on their actual financial situation, including the value of the voucher

**Significance**: First successful class action challenging algorithmic tenant screening as racially discriminatory. Established that failure to account for voucher subsidies in scoring algorithms constitutes source-of-income and racial discrimination.

**Sources**:

### (B) Connecticut Fair Housing Center v. CoreLogic -- FHA LIABILITY FOR SCREENING COMPANIES

- **Full name**: Connecticut Fair Housing Center, et al. v. CoreLogic Rental Property Solutions, LLC
- **Case No.**: 3:18-cv-00705
- **Court**: U.S. District Court, District of Connecticut (trial); Second Circuit (appeal)
- **Filed**: April 24, 2018
- **Bench trial concluded**: November 4, 2022
- **District court ruling**: July 20, 2023
- **Appeal filed**: August 4, 2023
- **Oral arguments**: November 20, 2024

**Subject**: CoreLogic's "CrimSAFE" product -- automated criminal background screening for tenant applications

**Allegations**: CrimSAFE caused unjustified disparate impact based on race in tenant screening. Carmen Arroyo's case was central: she sought to move her disabled son Mikhail into her apartment after a 2015 accident left him unable to speak, walk, or care for himself. CrimSAFE denied her request based on a **dismissed shoplifting charge from 2014**. Mikhail was left in an institution for a year.

**District Court ruling**: CoreLogic is **not subject to the Fair Housing Act**

**Appeal status**:
- DOJ filed amicus brief (Nov 24, 2023) asking Second Circuit to remand with instructions for reconsidering FHA liability
- National Fair Housing Alliance filed amicus brief (Nov 22, 2023) asking for reversal
- Oral arguments held Nov 20, 2024; awaiting decision

**Significance**: Could establish whether tenant screening companies bear direct liability under the Fair Housing Act -- a question with massive implications for the entire industry.

### (C) FTC/CFPB v. TransUnion Rental Screening Solutions

- **Settlement date**: October 18, 2023
- **Order terminated**: November 3, 2025 (obligations fulfilled)
- **Total**: **$15 million** ($11 million consumer redress + $4 million civil penalty)

**Findings**:
- TransUnion Rental Screening Solutions (TURSS) failed to follow reasonable procedures to:
  - Prevent **multiple entries for the same eviction case** (double-counting)
  - Accurately report **disposition of eviction cases**
  - Accurately **label monetary amounts** (disputed amounts labeled as "Judgment Amount" suggesting court ruling)
  - Prevent inclusion of **sealed eviction records**
- TURSS produced inaccurate/incomplete eviction records that hampered consumers' ability to obtain housing
- Of 2,953 disputes containing eviction records (Oct 2016-Oct 2018), **2,526 resulted in corrections** (85.5% error rate on disputed records)

**Outcome**: Required to ensure maximum possible accuracy of tenant screening reports and provide complete disclosures. TransUnion paid the $5M penalty plus redress payments and implemented injunctive relief.

### (D) FTC v. RealPage (FCRA Violations)

- **Date**: October 2018
- **Settlement**: **$3 million**

**Findings**: RealPage matched criminal records using only last name + non-exact first name/DOB. A search for "Anthony Jones, born 10/15/1967" would match "Antony Jones," "Antonio Jones," and even "Antoinette Jones." One background check company produced **11,000 inaccurate renter background reports between 2014 and 2019**. Prospective tenants falsely associated with other people's criminal records.

## 2. DOJ ANTITRUST -- ALGORITHMIC RENT PRICING

### (A) United States v. RealPage, Inc. (DOJ Civil Action)

- **Case No.**: 24-cv-710
- **Court**: Middle District of North Carolina
- **Filed**: August 23, 2024
- **Co-plaintiffs**: Attorneys General of North Carolina, California, Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Oregon, Tennessee, and Washington
- **Amended complaint** (January 2025): Added six major multifamily property owners/managers as co-defendants
- **Proposed settlement filed**: November 24, 2025

**Allegations**: RealPage contracted with competing landlords who agreed to share nonpublic, competitively sensitive information about rental rates and lease terms. This data trained and ran RealPage's algorithmic pricing software (YieldStar), which generated rental rate recommendations based on competitors' information -- amounting to per se price-fixing. Reports estimated renters paid **5-12% more** than they would in a competitive market.

**Settlement terms** (no financial penalty, no admission of wrongdoing):
1. RealPage must ensure algorithms do **not use competitors' nonpublic, competitively sensitive info** for runtime pricing
2. Limit model training to **historic data aged 12+ months** with no active lease data
3. **No geographic effects narrower than state level**
4. Remove/redesign features that **limited price decreases** or aligned pricing between competitors
5. **Stop conducting market surveys** collecting competitively sensitive information
6. Accept **court-appointed monitor** (7-year term; DOJ may end after 4 years)

## 3. EMPLOYMENT / AI HIRING CASES

### (A) EEOC v. iTutorGroup -- FIRST EEOC AI ENFORCEMENT

- **Case No.**: 1:22-cv-02565
- **Court**: Eastern District of New York
- **Filed**: 2022
- **Resolved**: Consent decree August 2023
- **Settlement**: **$365,000**

**Findings**: iTutorGroup's hiring software **automatically rejected** female applicants aged 55+ and male applicants aged 60+ in violation of the Age Discrimination in Employment Act (ADEA). Discovered when an applicant submitted two identical applications differing only in birth date. 200+ qualified applicants rejected.

**Significance**: First-ever EEOC enforcement action involving discriminatory use of AI in the workplace.

**Sources**:

### (B) Mobley v. Workday -- AI VENDOR DIRECT LIABILITY

- **Case No.**: 3:23-cv-00770-RFL
- **Court**: Northern District of California
- **Filed**: February 21, 2023
- **Plaintiff**: Derek Mobley, African American man over 40 with a disability
- **Defendant**: Workday, Inc.

**Allegations**: Mobley submitted 150+ job applications through employers using Workday's AI-powered screening tools over nine months. He received a rejection email at 1:30 AM on a weekend -- evidence no human reviewed his application. Alleges Workday's tools discriminate based on race, age, and disability in violation of Title VII, ADEA, and ADA.

**Novel legal theory**: AI vendor can be **directly liable** for employment discrimination under "agent" theory -- the vendor is not merely a tool but an active participant in discriminatory decision-making.

**Key developments**:
- **EEOC amicus brief** (April 9, 2024): Supported plaintiff's theory that AI vendors face direct liability under Title VII, ADA, and ADEA. First time EEOC argued vendors using AI tools are subject to federal anti-discrimination laws
- **May 16, 2025**: Court granted **preliminary collective certification**
- **Potential scope**: Workday represented that "1.1 billion applications were rejected" using its tools -- collective could include "hundreds of millions" of members

**Significance**: First case establishing precedent that AI hiring tool vendors can face direct employment discrimination liability, not just the employers who use them.

**Sources**:
- [Seyfarth Shaw analysis](https://www.seyfarth.com/news-insights/mobley-v-workday-court-holds-ai-service-providers-could-be-directly-liable-for-employment-discrimination-under-agent-theory.html)

### (C) Kistler v. Eightfold AI -- SECRET SCORING OF WORKERS

- **Case No.**: C26-00214
- **Court**: Contra Costa County Superior Court, California
- **Filed**: January 20, 2026
- **Counsel**: Former EEOC chair Jenny R. Yang; nonprofit Towards Justice; Outten & Golden LLP

**Allegations**: Eightfold AI scraped personal data on **over 1 billion workers** from social media profiles, location data, internet activity, and tracking data -- far beyond what candidates actually submitted in job applications. Its AI generated "Match Scores" ranking applicants 0-5. Lower-ranked candidates were filtered out **before any human being ever saw their applications**.

**Legal basis**: Fair Credit Reporting Act (FCRA) violations -- compiling "consumer reports" for employment decisions without the disclosures, consent, and dispute mechanisms required by law.

**Employers named**: Microsoft, PayPal (as users of the tool)

**Key distinction**: The lawsuit **does not claim the algorithm was biased** -- it claims **the algorithm existed in secret**. The entire scoring and filtering system operated without candidates' knowledge or consent.

**Sources**:
- [Outten & Golden press release](https://www.outtengolden.com/newsroom/landmark-class-action-accuses-eightfold-ai-of-illegally-producing-hidden-credit-reports-on-job-applicants)

### United States v. Meta Platforms, Inc.

- **Court**: Southern District of New York
- **Filed**: June 21, 2022
- **Settled**: June 27, 2022
- **Civil penalty**: **$115,054** (maximum under Fair Housing Act at time)

**Allegations**: Meta's housing advertising system discriminated based on race, color, religion, sex, disability, familial status, and national origin through:

## 10. INTERNATIONAL / EU REGULATORY ACTIONS

### (A) CJEU SCHUFA Ruling -- Case C-634/21 (December 7, 2023) -- LANDMARK

The Court of Justice of the European Union ruled that automated credit scoring by a credit reference agency (SCHUFA) constitutes **"automated individual decision-making"** within the meaning of GDPR Article 22.

**Key holdings**:
- Credit agencies must comply with GDPR Art. 22 protections: right to human intervention, right to express views, right to challenge decisions
- Organizations **cannot invoke trade secrets** to deny data subjects access to information about how their scores are calculated (though not required to disclose the algorithm itself)
- Art. 22 obligations fall on the **credit reference agency**, not just the lender
- Allowed only in limited situations: contractual necessity, specific EU/member state law justification, or explicit consent

**Significance**: Directly applicable to tenant screening, insurance scoring, and any algorithmic scoring system used in the EU. Establishes that the entity creating the score -- not just the entity using it -- bears responsibility.

**Sources**:
- [CJEU judgment](https://www.julia-project.eu/database/case-law/216)
- [Bird & Bird analysis](https://www.twobirds.com/en/insights/2023/global/key-takeaways-from-the-schufa-case-of-the-cjeu)
- [IAPP analysis](https://iapp.org/news/a/key-takeaways-from-the-cjeus-recent-automated-decision-making-rulings)

### (B) EU AI Act (entered into force August 1, 2024)

**Implementation timeline**:
- **February 2, 2025**: Prohibited AI systems must be discontinued; AI literacy obligations take effect
- **August 2, 2025**: General-purpose AI model providers must comply with transparency requirements
- **August 2, 2026**: Full applicability for high-risk AI systems

**Key provisions for scoring**:
- **Social scoring by governments: PROHIBITED**
- Credit scoring, hiring algorithms, insurance risk assessment: classified as **HIGH-RISK**
- High-risk systems must undergo conformity assessment, EU database registration, risk management, and human oversight
- Applies to safety-critical sectors and specific use cases including hiring processes, credit scoring, critical infrastructure, and border control

- Applications rejected despite applicants demonstrating good creditworthiness
- Cited lack of transparency and accountability in AI-driven processes

## 11. INVESTIGATIVE JOURNALISM

### The Markup "Locked Out" Series (2020-2021)

- Joint investigation with The New York Times into automated tenant screening industry
- Key findings:
  - Tenant background checks "widely used and often rife with inaccuracies"
  - Screeners use "wild card" matching algorithms that saddle people with others' criminal records
  - People with common names (especially Latinos) particularly at risk
  - **Marco Fernandez case**: Military service member with top-secret security clearance in Maryland denied housing because automated screening matched him with Mario Fernandez Santana -- who is on a federal watch list, lives in Mexico, and has a different date of birth
  - Analyzed ~150 cases from lawsuits against tenant screening companies
- **Impact**:
  - Cited by U.S. Senators questioning regulators about tenant screening oversight (March 2021)

## 12. CONGRESSIONAL & LEGISLATIVE ACTIVITY

### (A) Preventing the Algorithmic Facilitation of Rental Housing Cartels Act

- **Bill**: S. 3692 (Senate) / H.R. 8622 (House), 118th Congress (2024)
- **Sponsors**: Sens. Peter Welch (D-VT) and Ron Wyden (D-OR); Rep. Becca Balint (House)
- **Purpose**: Would make it **unlawful** for housing providers to contract for algorithmic revenue management services like RealPage's YieldStar. Designates such arrangements a **per se violation of federal antitrust laws** under the Sherman Act
- **Target**: Companies like RealPage and Yardi that "help landlords coordinate prices to increase rental rates" (estimated 5-12% rent increases)
- **Status**: Referred to Senate Judiciary Committee; **did not pass**

### (B) Algorithmic Accountability Act of 2025

- **Bill**: S.2164 / H.R.5511
- Third iteration of the bill (previously introduced in 2019, 2022)
- Requires impact assessments for automated decision systems
- FTC enforcement mechanism
- **Status**: Pending

### (C) Senate Banking Committee Hearing (March 12, 2024)

- Examined proposals to address housing discrimination
- Testimony on algorithmic discrimination in rental housing and tenant screening

### (D) FTC/CFPB Joint Request for Information (February 2023)

- FTC-2023-0024
- Sought public comment on how background screening may shut renters out of housing
- Comments informed enforcement and policy actions
